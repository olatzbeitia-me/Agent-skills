# Module 01: The essence of executive presence

**What this module covers:** what drives executive presence, how it shapes leadership effectiveness, how to prioritise the dimensions that matter most for you, how to analyse leadership styles against it, and how to judge when to conform and when to act as a changemaker.

## 1. Key concepts

### Presence is a judgement other people make

Executive presence is the judgement senior people make about whether you can be trusted with consequential decisions and with people. Because it is a perception, it can be developed. Because it is a perception, it can also be distorted by bias — and UK research is unusually clear about how.

### The two best-known models (both US practitioner models)

- **Hewlett's three pillars.** Sylvia Ann Hewlett's Center for Talent Innovation survey (fielded 2012; 268 senior executives among nearly 4,000 respondents) found 67% of senior executives named **gravitas** (confidence under pressure, decisiveness, integrity, vision) as the core of presence, 28% **communication** and 5% **appearance**. Appearance works as a filter: get it wrong and people stop hearing the rest.
- **The Bates model.** Bates Communications describes fifteen facets in three groups: **character** (authenticity, integrity, concern, restraint, humility), **substance** (practical wisdom, confidence, composure, resonance, vision) and **style** (appearance or demeanour, intentionality, inclusiveness, interactivity, assertiveness). Its value is turning a vague quality into behaviours people can give feedback on. Its validation is vendor-reported.

Use these as vocabularies, not as measured truths.

### Matching style to the amount of change (Henley Business School)

Victor Dulewicz and Malcolm Higgs at Henley developed the Leadership Dimensions Questionnaire, which measures fifteen leadership dimensions and the amount of change a leader faces. From those profiles they describe three styles:

| Style | What it looks like | Context it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Engaging** | High empowerment and involvement; builds capability and commitment in others | Radical, transformational change |
| **Involving** | Shares decisions and builds alignment while keeping direction | Significant but not radical change |
| **Goal-oriented** | Clear targets, delivery and control | Relatively stable context |

Their research, including UK private-sector managers and the Royal Navy and Royal Air Force, linked the leadership dimensions to leader performance and follower commitment, with some support for the idea that fit between style and change context matters. For a scale-up marketing leader, the implication is direct: in a company doubling every year, a purely goal-oriented style ("hit the lead number") under-delivers on commitment, while an engaging style without any goal discipline loses the board. Most scale-ups need engaging or involving leadership with goal-oriented habits underneath.

Daniel Goleman's six styles (coercive, authoritative, affiliative, democratic, pacesetting, coaching; US) remain a useful vocabulary, especially the warning that **pacesetting** ("I'll fix the deck myself") damages climate when overused. In small scale-up teams, pacesetting is the most common default and the most costly to presence: the founder sees you working at the wrong level.

### Authenticity and growing into a role

Herminia Ibarra (London Business School) argues in "The authenticity paradox" (2015) that a rigid idea of being "true to yourself" can stop leaders growing into bigger roles. New behaviours feel fake at first; that discomfort is often a sign of growth. Her recommendation is to borrow from several role models, set learning goals rather than only performance goals, and treat your leadership identity as work in progress. This matters more in a scale-up, where the role you were hired for may be a different role within a year.

### Conform or change?

- **Idiosyncrasy credits** (Hollander, 1958; US). Groups grant latitude to deviate from norms to people who have shown competence and met core expectations. Earn credits by delivering what the organisation values before spending them on challenging it.
- **Tempered radicals** (Meyerson and Scully, 1995; US). People who want to change an organisation while succeeding in it use small wins, framing change in the organisation's own language, and alliances, rather than open revolt.

In a scale-up, credits are earned and spent faster. One visible commercial win in the first 60 days can buy latitude that would take a year in a large company — and one public miss can spend it.

### The bias you should not skip: UK evidence

Presence norms often encode the habits of the people who have historically held power. In Britain, this is documented, not just suspected:

- **Accent.** In the Accent Bias in Britain project (Queen Mary University of London, University of York), a representative sample of 848 people in England judged recorded "candidates" for a trainee solicitor role. There was persistent bias against some accents, particularly Southern working-class varieties, moderated by listener age and by the content of what was said (Levon, Sharma, Watt, Cardoso and Ye, 2021). The encouraging finding: professional recruiters were better able to focus on the quality of answers, and simply raising awareness of accent bias before judging candidates reduced it.
- **Class and "polish".** Sam Friedman and Daniel Laurison's *The Class Ceiling* (London School of Economics, 2019; 200 interviews across television, accountancy, architecture and acting) found that people from working-class backgrounds earn less than privileged colleagues in the same elite occupations, and that unwritten behavioural codes and informal sponsorship help explain the gap — including in creative industries that present themselves as open.
- **Progression without a performance gap.** Research by the Bridge Group for the City of London Corporation found 89% of senior roles in UK financial services were held by people from higher socio-economic backgrounds, and that people from lower socio-economic backgrounds progressed around 25% more slowly with no link to performance. The response from the sector was framed as "performance over polish".

The skill does not ask learners simply to conform. It asks them to separate norms that serve the organisation (composure under pressure, clear decisions, commercial judgement) from norms that are about fitting a historical type (accent, a particular way of dressing or socialising), and to decide deliberately when to adapt and when to challenge — ideally once they hold enough credits to do so safely. Leaders who judge others' presence should take the accent research at its word: name the bias before you assess.

## 2. Practical applications for scale-up marketing leaders

- **Presence with the founder.** In scale-ups, presence is mainly judged by the founder, the chief executive and investors. It usually rests on three things: commercial clarity (you can talk about pipeline, customer acquisition cost, payback and runway without notes), decisiveness with incomplete data, and composure when things break. Formal polish matters much less than in a large company; speed and substance matter more.
- **Gravitas with the board.** Board members and investors judge marketing on whether its numbers are honest and its plan connects to the next funding milestone. Presenting one clear recommendation, two options and the trade-off, with the risk stated, is gravitas in practice.
- **Composure in a crisis.** A failed launch, a social media storm or a lost flagship customer is where presence is made or lost. Restraint means a calm, accurate first response, not the fastest one.
- **Brand and campaign leadership.** Vision means connecting the campaign to the company's growth plan and next milestone, not only to the creative idea.
- **Agencies and freelancers.** Presence means clear, decisive briefs and feedback. Outside partners learn quickly whether your "yes" survives the founder's next opinion.
- **Leading creative and analytical people.** Inclusiveness means drawing out the quieter analyst in creative discussions and the creative in data reviews.

## 3. Diagnostic questions

1. In the last leadership or board meeting, was I asked for my judgement or only for an update?
2. Which three Bates facets would my founder or chief executive say are my strongest? Which are weakest?
3. How much change is my company facing (Dulewicz and Higgs), and does my style fit it?
4. What is my default under pressure? Do I slip into pacesetting?
5. Which presence norms here are about competence, and which are about fitting a type?
6. How many credits do I hold? What have I delivered that the founder visibly values?
7. Where am I holding back a behaviour because it "doesn't feel like me" — is that values, or habit?

## 4. Templates

### Template A: Executive presence priority matrix

Step 1. Rate yourself 1–5 on up to eight facets; ask two people (one senior, one peer) to rate you on the five you think matter most.

Step 2. Rate each facet's importance to your next career goal (1–5). A first chief marketing officer role in a Series B company, for example, weights practical wisdom, decisiveness and composure highly.

Step 3. Pick the two highest "gap × importance" scores and define one observable behaviour for each:

| Priority facet | Behaviour I will practise | Where | Who will tell me how it landed | Review date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| e.g. Practical wisdom | Open every budget discussion with the revenue outcome and the trade-off before any marketing metric | Monthly business review with the founder | Finance lead | 6 weeks |

## 5. This week

1. Complete the priority matrix self-rating (30 minutes).
2. Ask two colleagues: "In leadership meetings, what's one thing I do that increases your confidence in my judgement, and one thing that reduces it?"
3. Before your next founder or board conversation, prepare three answers to the hardest commercial question you could be asked.
4. Spot one moment this week when you default to pacesetting, and delegate it instead.

## 6. Evidence notes, scale-up and European variation

- Executive presence models are descriptive and come mainly from US practitioners. Hewlett's percentages come from one survey; Bates's validation is vendor-reported. The Henley style research is UK academic work with practitioner use; its evidence for style–context fit is supportive rather than conclusive.
- The UK bias research is strong for what it measures (experimental accent judgements; large interview and survey studies on class) but does not measure "executive presence" directly. Use it to show how presence judgements can be shaped by factors unrelated to performance.
- **Scale-up:** presence is judged by fewer people, faster, and against commercial substance more than polish. Credits are earned and spent quickly. Informality is often the norm, but informal codes still exist and can exclude people.
- **Larger company (for contrast):** composure and polish in formal forums count for more; presence is judged by more people over a longer period.
- **European variation:** assertiveness, formality and how openly disagreement is expressed differ by country (Meyer, *The Culture Map*). A leader judged "measured" in London may be read as evasive in Amsterdam and as abrupt in parts of Asia. Seek feedback from raters in each key market.
- Do not coach anyone to change features such as an accent or cultural style unless they choose to and understand the trade-offs.
