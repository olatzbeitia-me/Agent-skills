## 1. Key concepts

### Psychological safety and "real teams"

Amy Edmondson (Harvard; US research) defined team psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. Her 1999 study of 51 work teams found it was associated with learning behaviour, which in turn was associated with performance. The point often missed: safety matters because it lets people raise problems, ask for help and admit mistakes — not because it makes work comfortable. Edmondson pairs it with high standards.

The best synthesis is Frazier and colleagues' 2017 meta-analysis (136 samples, more than 22,000 individuals). Psychological safety correlated with task performance (.43), learning behaviour (.62) and engagement (.45) at the individual level. Two cautions: the link with *objective* performance was far weaker (.07), and most studies were cross-sectional, so cause and effect are not established.

The strongest UK counterpart comes from Michael West (Aston, then Lancaster University and The King's Fund). His team distinguished **real teams** from **co-acting groups** — people who call themselves a team but do not function as one. Real teams have three features:

1. **Shared objectives** that members know and agree.
2. **Genuine interdependence** — members need each other to do the work.
3. **Reflexivity** — the team regularly stops to review how it is working and changes course.

In large NHS samples, Lyubovnikova, West, Dawson and Carter (2015) found that staff in real teams witnessed fewer errors, had fewer work-related injuries, experienced less harassment and were less likely to intend to leave; hospitals with more real-team working had lower patient mortality and sickness absence. It is healthcare data, and correlational, but it is large-scale and gives a marketing leader something concrete to build: shared objectives, real interdependence and a regular reflection habit.

West also describes **compassionate leadership** as four behaviours: **attending** (being present and listening), **understanding** (building a shared understanding of the problem), **empathising** (feeling with someone without being overwhelmed) and **helping** (removing obstacles and securing resources). He stresses it is not a soft option; it is a practical discipline for leading under pressure.

### Structured feedback

Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes (US and Israeli researchers) found feedback improved performance on average (d = .41), but more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse. Their explanation: feedback helps when it keeps attention on the task and how to improve, and backfires when it shifts attention to the person. This is why the skill builds feedback around observable behaviour:

- **Situation–Behaviour–Impact** (Center for Creative Leadership): describe the specific situation, the observable behaviour and its impact, then ask about the person's intent rather than assuming it.
- **Feedforward** (Marshall Goldsmith): ask for and offer suggestions for the future. Useful with founders, investors and senior peers who would resist critique of the past.

### Feedback across European cultures

Erin Meyer's *Culture Map* (INSEAD) places countries on an "evaluating" scale for negative feedback. British managers tend to soften criticism with "downgraders" ("a little", "slightly", "not quite there yet") and deliver it privately. Dutch, German, French and Danish colleagues tend to be more direct and may use "upgraders" ("this is completely wrong"). In a London–Amsterdam–Berlin marketing team, the same intended message can land as too vague or too harsh.

Meyer's practical advice: work out whether you are more or less direct than the person in front of you, then adjust by one step. Do not try to imitate a culture you do not know well; you will overshoot. Agree team norms explicitly ("in reviews, we say what isn't working in plain words, then we say what would fix it").

### Developing a team that is growing fast

- **Accidental managers.** The Chartered Management Institute's 2023 YouGov survey of 4,500 UK workers and managers found 82% of people who take on management roles have had no formal management training. Those with training were more likely to trust their team, lead change confidently and call out poor behaviour. In scale-ups, people are often promoted into management within months; the marketing leader frequently has to provide the training.
- **Collaborative overload.** Cross, Rebele and Grant (2016; US) found that 20–35% of value-added collaborations in the organisations they studied came from 3–5% of employees. In a small marketing team this is usually the marketing operations person, the one analyst, or the long-serving brand guardian. They burn out quietly.
- **Creative and analytical specialists.** There is little robust research comparing their development directly, so this is practice guidance. Creatives usually grow through stretch briefs, exposure to customers and protected time for craft; analysts through owning a commercial question, exposure to finance and the board, and learning to communicate uncertainty. Both need to see how their work connects to revenue.

### Trust during change

Frances Frei and Anne Morriss (2020; US) argue trust rests on **logic** (confidence in your judgement), **empathy** (belief you care) and **authenticity** (belief you are the real you); when trust breaks, one of the three has "wobbled". In scale-ups, change is constant — hiring sprees, reorganisations every few quarters, layoffs after a missed round, founder departures — and leaders most often wobble on logic (not explaining why) and empathy (moving too fast to notice who is hurt).

The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standards list **change** as one of six sources of work-related stress (with demands, control, support, relationships and role). They give a recognised UK framework for consulting people about change.

### The UK marketing picture right now

Marketing Week's 2026 Career and Salary Survey (2,350 UK respondents) found 65.3% of marketers had felt overwhelmed in the past year, 60.7% undervalued and 55.1% emotionally exhausted — and 42.5% did not feel able to tell their manager or the wider business how they felt. In the 2025 survey, marketers in organisations of 250 people or fewer were slightly more likely to feel overwhelmed (59.8%) than those in larger firms (57.1%). These are self-selected survey figures, but the direction is consistent: a marketing team's silence is not evidence that it is fine.

## 2. Practical applications in a scale-up marketing team

- **Make the team a real team.** Write three shared objectives on one page; make sure at least two depend on more than one person; hold a 20-minute "how are we working?" review every fortnight. This is West's model at the scale of five people.
- **Campaign and test post-mortems.** Run them as learning reviews: what we expected, what happened, why, what we will change. The leader names one of their own decisions that did not work, first. In a scale-up running weekly experiments, this habit is the difference between learning and hiding.
- **Report bad news early.** Make it explicitly safe for the analyst to say a channel is not working before the founder or an investor finds out. Thank people publicly for early bad news.
- **Creative reviews.** Separate generating ideas from judging them. Ask "what is strongest here?" before "what's wrong?". Junior creatives self-censor when the founder is in the room.
- **Agencies and freelancers.** Scale-ups rely heavily on outside help. Agencies that fear losing a small, volatile client tell you what you want to hear. A quarterly "what should we stop doing?" conversation, with explicit protection for candour, improves the advice you get.
- **Sales and marketing.** Use feedforward on lead-quality disputes: "What would make next month's leads more useful to you?" beats relitigating last quarter.
- **Layoffs, reorganisations and founder changes.** Explain the logic, including what is not decided. Speak to affected people individually before any group announcement. Hold a follow-up within a week. Do not let the all-hands be the first time anyone hears.

## 3. Diagnostic questions

1. When did someone in my team last tell me I was wrong in front of others? How did I respond?
2. Is my team a real team — shared objectives, real interdependence, regular reflection — or a group of people who report to me?
3. Who are the two or three people everyone leans on? Are they recognised, and are they overloaded?
4. In my last three pieces of feedback, did I describe behaviour and impact, or the person?
5. Am I more or less direct than each person I manage? How do I know?
6. In the last change I led, which wobbled most — logic, empathy or authenticity?
7. If 42% of marketers would not tell their manager they are struggling, who in my team might that be?

## 4. Templates

### Template A: Team safety and real-team pulse

Ask the team to rate each statement from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), anonymously. The first four are adapted from Edmondson's scale; the last three reflect West's real-team features. Phrase them in your own words.

| Statement | Average | Range | Action in the next 30 days |
|---|---|---|---|
| If I make a mistake here, it is not held against me | | | e.g. open next post-mortem with my own mistake |
| I can raise problems and tough issues | | | |
| It is safe to take a risk here | | | |
| My skills are valued and used | | | |
| I know and agree with our team's objectives | | | |
| I need other people in the team to do my work well | | | |
| We regularly review how we work and change it | | | |

*Artefact:* a one-page baseline with three dated actions. Re-run it in 90 days, or after any major change.

### Template B: Structured feedback support plan

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Person and role | e.g. Content and social lead |
| Situation | Specific and dated: "In Tuesday's pipeline review with the founder…" |
| Behaviour (observable) | "…you showed engagement metrics but not the demo requests the founder had asked about…" |
| Impact | "…so the founder questioned the whole channel and the decision on next month's budget was delayed." |
| Their intent (ask, don't assume) | "What were you aiming for?" Record the answer. |
| Directness check | Am I more or less direct than this person? One-step adjustment I will make |
| Agreed future behaviour | "Check the decision the founder needs 48 hours before any review." |
| Support I will provide | Template, pairing with the analyst, a preview slot with me |
| Strength to build on | "Your storytelling is strong; pair it with the one number they need." |
| Review date and success indicator | Two weeks; next review proceeds without data challenges |

*Artefact:* a completed plan shared with the person after the conversation, so it is an agreed record.

## 5. This week

1. Run the seven-item pulse (ten minutes, anonymous form).
2. Write your team's three shared objectives on one page and test them with the team.
3. Give one piece of Situation–Behaviour–Impact feedback and note how it landed, including whether you adjusted your directness.
4. In your next review, name one of your own decisions that did not work before anyone else speaks.

## 6. Evidence notes, scale-up and European variation

- **Strong:** psychological safety's association with learning and engagement. **Weaker:** its direct link to objective performance. **Large-scale but healthcare and correlational:** West's real-team findings. **Not peer-reviewed:** Google's Project Aristotle; treat as corroboration only.
- **Practitioner frameworks:** Situation–Behaviour–Impact, feedforward and Frei and Morriss's triangle are consistent with Kluger and DeNisi but not themselves tested in controlled trials. Meyer's scales are a field-tested practitioner model based on her research and teaching at INSEAD; use them to compare groups, not to stereotype individuals.
- **Scale-up:** with no human resources function, the leader's behaviour is most of the team's experience of the company. Expect to coach first-time managers yourself and to set feedback norms explicitly. Safety is tested hardest after a missed target or funding round, when the temptation to find someone to blame is strongest.
- **Larger company (for contrast):** wider culture and processes, such as forced ranking, can undermine what a team leader builds; the leader has less influence but more support.
- **Regulated sectors** (financial services, gambling, alcohol, healthcare): make the route for raising compliance concerns explicit; safety to speak up is a regulatory matter, not just a cultural one.
