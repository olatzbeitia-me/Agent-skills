# Module 02: Developing executive presence throughout a career

**What this module covers:** applying emotional, social and wellbeing intelligence across career moments, transitions and conflict; spotting opportunities for social intelligence and emotional awareness; sustaining presence through transitions — including the repeated transitions of a scale-up; managing workplace conflict; drafting support plans using structured feedback; and building self-awareness into everyday leadership.

## 1. Key concepts

### Emotional intelligence: two traditions, one practical conclusion

- **Trait emotional intelligence** (K. V. Petrides, University College London). Emotional intelligence as "emotional self-efficacy": how good we believe we are at perceiving, understanding, regulating and expressing emotions. It sits within personality and is measured by self-report, most often the Trait Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire (fifteen facets, four factors: wellbeing, self-control, emotionality, sociability). A study in a European multinational found trait emotional intelligence helped distinguish people in leadership positions from those who were not, alongside cognitive ability and gender (Siegling, Nielsen and Petrides, 2014).
- **Ability emotional intelligence** (Mayer, Salovey and Caruso; US). A set of abilities — perceiving, using, understanding and managing emotions — measured by a performance test.
- **Competency models** (Goleman and Boyatzis; US) mix emotional and social competencies and are measured by multi-rater feedback.

**What the evidence says.** O'Boyle and colleagues' 2011 meta-analysis found correlations of about .24 to .30 between the main streams of emotional intelligence measurement and job performance. Whether emotional intelligence adds much beyond personality and general intelligence is contested: ability measures add very little; self-report measures add more but overlap heavily with personality (Joseph and Newman, 2010). Petrides's own position is that trait emotional intelligence is best understood as part of personality rather than a separate "intelligence".

**Practical conclusion:** treat emotional intelligence as learnable behaviours — noticing, naming, regulating, reading others, responding — and check them with other people's feedback rather than self-assessment alone. Avoid claims that emotional intelligence "matters more than IQ".

### Social intelligence

Goleman and Boyatzis (2008; US) describe social intelligence as the interpersonal competencies that inspire others: empathy, attunement, organisational awareness, influence, developing others and teamwork. Treat it as practitioner synthesis; some of the neuroscience framing in popular writing goes beyond the evidence. Michael West's compassionate leadership behaviours (attending, understanding, empathising, helping — see module 00) are a more concrete UK version of the same idea.

### Wellbeing intelligence

This means recognising your own and your team's capacity limits and managing them deliberately. The World Health Organization classifies burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. In the UK, the Health and Safety Executive's Management Standards (demands, control, support, relationships, role, change) give a recognised structure for assessing pressure. Presence depends on composure, and composure is the first thing to go when a leader is depleted. Marketing Week's 2026 survey found 55.1% of UK marketers emotionally exhausted; senior marketers are not exempt.

### Self-awareness

Tasha Eurich's research (US) separates **internal** self-awareness (clarity about your own values, reactions and impact) from **external** self-awareness (understanding how others see you); the two are only weakly related. She found "what" questions ("What situations bring out my worst?") build self-awareness better than "why" questions, which invite plausible but invented explanations. Her widely quoted claim that only 10–15% of people are self-aware comes from her own studies; treat it as indicative.

### Transitions — and why scale-ups make them repeat

- **The first 90 days.** Michael Watkins (*The First 90 Days*, 2003, updated 2013; US practitioner work) argues new leaders should first diagnose the situation they face — start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment or sustaining success — because each needs a different approach. Secure early wins, agree expectations with your manager, and build a coalition.
- **Working identity.** Herminia Ibarra (*Working Identity*, 2003; London Business School/INSEAD) shows people change careers by experimenting — new activities, new networks, reworking their story — rather than planning in the abstract.
- **Growth crises.** Greiner's model (see `scale-up-context.md`) predicts that each phase of growth ends in a crisis — leadership, then autonomy, then control. Each crisis changes what the marketing leader's job is.
- **Leaders who do not scale.** Index Ventures' study of 210 venture-backed companies found that very few functional leaders are star performers at every stage, and that companies often go through several generations of leadership in core roles.

The implication for a scale-up marketing leader: you will go through a transition every time the company crosses a growth threshold, even if your title does not change. A leader who treats each threshold as a new "first 90 days" — rediagnosing, renegotiating expectations, handing over parts of the job — is far more likely to grow with the company than one who waits to be told the role has changed.

### Conflict

- **Cost and timing (UK).** Acas research by Saundry and Urwin (2021) estimated workplace conflict cost UK organisations £28.5 billion a year — more than £1,000 per employee — with close to 10 million people experiencing conflict at work. Where conflict escalated to formal procedures, costs were more than three times those of informal resolution. The message is to act early.
- **Managers as cause and cure (UK).** The Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development's conflict research (2020 report, and later employer and employee surveys) found line managers are as likely to make conflict worse as to resolve it. In the later survey of around 2,000 employers and 5,000 employees, 49% of employers admitted managers can cause conflict in their teams, and only about a third of employees who had experienced conflict felt it had been resolved.
- **Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument** (US). Five modes — competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating — on two axes, assertiveness and cooperativeness. None is always right; choose deliberately. Scale-up marketing leaders often over-use accommodating with the founder and sales, and competing with agencies.
- **Interest-based negotiation** (Fisher and Ury, *Getting to Yes*, 1981; US). Separate people from the problem, focus on interests not positions, generate options, use objective criteria.
- **Disagreement across cultures.** Meyer's *Culture Map* notes that French and Dutch colleagues are often comfortable with open, confrontational debate in meetings that British colleagues may experience as conflict. Agree how the team disagrees before it matters.

## 2. Practical applications for scale-up marketing leaders

- **From doer to leader.** The first identity shift is from producing the work to leading the people who produce it. Expect it to feel inauthentic at first (Ibarra, 2015). In a team of three you will still do some of the work; decide which parts, and protect time for the rest.
- **Each growth threshold.** At 50, 150 and 300 people (or at each funding round), rediagnose. Ask the founder: "What do you need from marketing that is different from six months ago?" Write down what you will hand over.
- **Joining from a corporate.** Diagnose before acting. A company that has just lost its last marketing leader is a turnaround or realignment, not "sustaining success". Resist importing process in the first 60 days; earn credits first.
- **Conflict with the founder over the marketing plan.** Usually an interests problem: the founder cares about a specific market story or investor narrative; you care about channel economics. Make the interests explicit and agree the criteria for deciding.
- **Conflict with sales over lead quality.** Agree a shared definition with objective criteria (conversion by lead source over two quarters). Kotler, Rackham and Krishnaswamy (2006; US) recommend shared funnel definitions and joint metrics.
- **Conflict between creative and analytical team members.** Name the shared purpose, then separate "what does the evidence say?" from "what does the brand need?".
- **Agency and freelancer disputes.** State the facts (missed deadline, overspend) without the story ("they don't care about us"), ask for their view, agree the fix.
- **Wellbeing in launch season and fundraising periods.** Plan recovery explicitly, watch the two or three people most at risk of overload, and model it yourself.

## 3. Diagnostic questions

1. What situation am I in (Watkins), and which growth crisis is the company feeling (Greiner)?
2. What has changed in what the founder needs from me in the last six months?
3. "What" question: what situations reliably bring out my least effective behaviour?
4. Which conflict mode do I default to with the founder, sales, finance, agencies and my team?
5. Who in my team is showing signs of strain, and what have I done about it?
6. What would my team say I am like under pressure? How do I know?
7. Which parts of my current job should someone else be doing in six months?

## 6. Evidence notes, scale-up and European variation

- **Emotional intelligence:** moderate links with performance are well established; its value beyond personality and intelligence is contested. Trait emotional intelligence is a well-developed UK research programme; its measures are self-report.
- **Transitions:** Watkins and Greiner are practitioner and stage models, not controlled findings. Index Ventures' findings are investor research, useful but not neutral.
- **Conflict:** the Acas figures are modelled estimates from 2018–19 data and are best used to show scale and the value of early, informal resolution.
- **Scale-up:** transitions repeat; the founder often acts as arbiter in conflicts; there may be no human resources function, so point to Acas guidance when anything becomes formal.
- **Larger company (for contrast):** fewer, larger transitions; more formal conflict processes; more support but slower resolution.
- **European variation:** comfort with open disagreement and directness differs by country (Meyer). In multi-country teams, agree explicit norms for debate and for escalation.
