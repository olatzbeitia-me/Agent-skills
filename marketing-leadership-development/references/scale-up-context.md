# Scale-up context: what changes, what does not

Read this early in any conversation. It sets the default setting for the whole skill and answers a question learners often ask: "Is leading marketing in a scale-up actually different from leading it in a large company, or is it the same job with fewer resources?"

The short answer: the underlying behavioural science is the same, but where power sits, how fast things move, how long a role stays the same shape, and what counts as evidence all change. Those differences change which behaviours earn trust.

## 1. What a scale-up is

The definition used by the UK's ScaleUp Institute comes from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's measure of a high-growth enterprise: average annualised growth of more than 20% a year over three years, in employees or turnover, with at least 10 employees at the start of the period. Eurostat's official European statistics use a lower 10% threshold, so European figures are not always comparable with UK ones.

Two practical points follow:

- **Most scale-ups are small.** The ScaleUp Institute's analysis of Office for National Statistics data (2020 review, 2018 data) found most UK scale-ups had between 10 and 50 employees and only 6% had more than 250. A "scale-up marketing leader" is often running a team of two to eight people.
- **Not every scale-up is a venture-backed technology company.** Family businesses, manufacturers and professional services firms can all meet the definition. This skill assumes the common case — founder-led, investor-backed, often technology-enabled — but check, because a family-owned scale-up has different power dynamics from one with a venture capital board seat.

## 2. How strong the evidence is

Be candid with learners about this. In their 2023 *Journal of Management Studies* special issue, Jansen, Heavey, Mom, Simsek and Zahra (Rotterdam School of Management and colleagues) describe research on scaling and high-growth firms as fragmented and lacking a central conceptual foundation. They frame scaling as a dynamic capability built on routines for expansion, replication and synchronisation.

So the scale-up guidance in this skill draws on four kinds of evidence, each with a different weight:

| Kind of evidence | Examples used here | How much weight it bears |
|---|---|---|
| Peer-reviewed research on growth and change | Jansen and colleagues (2023); Schou (2023) on identity fragmentation; Dulewicz and Higgs on matching leadership style to the amount of change | Moderate; often case-based or small samples |
| Large-scale UK survey data | ScaleUp Institute annual reviews; Chartered Management Institute; Marketing Week Career and Salary Survey | Good for describing what leaders report; not causal |
| Investor research and practitioner data | Index Ventures, *Scaling Through Chaos* (210 companies, 200,000 career profiles); Atomico, *State of European Tech* | Useful patterns; investors have a point of view |
| Classic stage models | Greiner (1972, updated 1998) | Widely used heuristic; not empirically tested as a sequence |

Where a recommendation rests on the lower rows, say so.

## 3. Growth stages and what they ask of a marketing leader

Larry Greiner's growth model (*Harvard Business Review*, 1972; updated 1998) describes companies growing through phases, each ending in a predictable crisis:

1. **Growth through creativity** → crisis of leadership (the founders cannot run everything; professional management is needed).
2. **Growth through direction** → crisis of autonomy (decisions bottleneck on a few people; others want authority).
3. **Growth through delegation** → crisis of control (units drift apart).
4. **Growth through coordination** → crisis of red tape.
5. **Growth through collaboration** (and, in the 1998 update, a sixth phase of growth through alliances).

Use it as a diagnostic, not a law. Most scale-up marketing leaders are hired at the first or second crisis: the founder has been doing marketing (or overseeing a freelancer) and now needs someone to own it, but has not yet given up control. That tension explains much of what learners describe as "the founder keeps rewriting my plan".

Index Ventures' research adds two findings that matter for a marketing leader's own career:

- From a founding team of ten, typically only two or three remain by the time the company reaches 1,000 employees.
- Very few functional leaders are star performers at every stage of growth; companies often go through several generations of leadership in core roles.

The practical message: a scale-up marketing role has a shelf life unless the leader deliberately grows with it. Module 02 treats this as a recurring transition, not a one-off.

## 4. What changes in a scale-up

| Dimension | Typical large enterprise | Typical scale-up | Behaviour that earns trust in a scale-up | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Where power sits | Formal role, budget ownership, committees | Proximity to the founder and chief executive; investors and board; informal influence | Earn the founder's confidence early; be explicit about decision rights rather than assuming them | Practitioner and stage-model evidence; Wasserman (2008) on founders' control motives |
| Speed and decision rights | Annual cycles, approval layers | Weekly or monthly; decisions reversed quickly | Decide with incomplete data, say what would change your mind, and close loops visibly | Practitioner |
| Role stability | Role stays broadly the same for years | Scope changes every six to twelve months; the company may outgrow the role | Treat each growth step as a transition; hand over parts of the job deliberately | Index Ventures; Greiner |
| What counts as evidence | Attribution models, brand tracking, finance sign-off | Pipeline, revenue, payback within the funding runway; quick experiments | Show results in the board's language (pipeline, customer acquisition cost, payback, runway) and run small tests before asking for big bets | Practitioner; Binet and Field on the cost of over-weighting short-term activation |
| Budget | Annual, defended through process | Linked to funding rounds and runway; can be cut overnight | Keep a ranked list of what you would cut and protect, ready before anyone asks | Practitioner |
| Team | Specialists, established managers, human resources support | Generalists, first-time managers, little or no human resources function | Coach first-time managers directly; make feedback routine because no process will do it for you | Chartered Management Institute (82% of new managers untrained); ScaleUp Institute |
| Culture and identity | Established; slow to shift | Still forming; can fragment as the company commercialises and doubles headcount | Repeat the story; rework it openly as the company changes | Schou (2023) |
| Conflict | Formal processes, human resources involvement | Few processes; the founder often arbitrates | Resolve early and informally; Acas found formal routes cost more than three times as much as informal ones | Acas (2021) |
| Networks that matter | Internal strategic network across divisions | External: investors, non-executive directors, peer groups, fractional executives, first customers | Build the external network deliberately; scale-up leaders rate peer networks and mentors highly | ScaleUp Institute |
| Communication | Cascades through layers | All-hands, written updates, investor updates; the founder's voice dominates | Write clearly enough that a new joiner next month understands the strategy without you | Practitioner; Schou (2023) |

## 5. What does not change

Learners moving between contexts sometimes over-correct and assume everything they knew is wrong. These findings hold regardless of company size:

- **Psychological safety** relates to learning behaviour and engagement (module 00).
- **Feedback aimed at the task works better than feedback aimed at the person** (module 00).
- **Consultation, rational persuasion and collaboration** are rated effective influence tactics across cultures; pressure tactics are not (module 05).
- **Strengths use** is associated with better performance and wellbeing (module 03).
- **Clear, answer-first writing** helps busy readers in any organisation (module 06).

What changes is how quickly you need to apply them, who your key audiences are, and how much structure exists to help you.

## 6. Moving from a corporate to a scale-up

The ScaleUp Institute's 2021 survey found that scale-up leaders looking to strengthen their senior teams sought people who had grown a business (34%) far more than people who had worked in a larger corporate (10%). That does not mean corporate experience is unwelcome; it means it has to be translated. Common translation points (practitioner observation, not tested evidence):

- **Process timing.** Corporate leaders often import process before the company needs it. Ask "what is the smallest structure that solves this month's problem?"
- **Waiting for permission.** In a scale-up, the absence of a "no" is often the only "yes" you will get. Decide, inform, and make it easy to reverse.
- **Hiring ahead of the stage.** Building the team the company will need in two years can burn runway now. Hire for the next 12 to 18 months and plan the step after.
- **Polish over pace.** A clear, rough plan shared on Tuesday usually beats a polished one shared in three weeks.
- **Doing and leading.** In a team of three, the leader also does the work. Protect a small amount of time for leading, or nobody will.

The reverse move — scale-up to corporate — has its own traps: underestimating stakeholder mapping, moving faster than the governance allows, and reading formal process as obstruction. Module 05 helps with both.

## 7. Working across European cultures

Many UK scale-ups have teams, customers or investors across Europe from early on. Atomico's *State of European Tech 2025* reported more than 4.3 million people working in venture-backed technology companies in Europe. Erin Meyer's *Culture Map* (INSEAD) is the most practical tool for leaders managing across these borders. Two of her scales matter most for this skill:

- **Evaluating (negative feedback).** British managers tend to soften criticism with "downgraders" ("a little", "slightly", "quite"); Dutch, German, French and Danish colleagues tend to give negative feedback more directly. A British "this is quite good, but…" may be heard in Amsterdam as praise. See module 00.
- **Persuading.** Meyer places Italy, France and Spain at the "principles-first" end (explain the reasoning and method before the conclusion), the UK, Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden in the middle, and the US, Canada and Australia at the "applications-first" end (start with the example and the practical outcome). See module 05.

Meyer's own caution applies: these are cultural tendencies for comparing groups, not predictions about any individual, and what matters is the relative position of the two people involved.
