# Module 05: Persuasion and influence

**What this module covers:** ethical influence rooted in emotional intelligence; sources and types of power and how they shape organisational politics; practical influence techniques; the ethical — and, in the UK, legal — limits of influence; and a plan for future influencing scenarios centred on organisational benefit.

## 1. Key concepts

### Politics is normal — British managers say so

David Buchanan (Cranfield School of Management) surveyed 250 British managers about organisational politics (*British Journal of Management*, 2008). Political behaviour was common. Most managers saw it as ethical and necessary, and linked it to effectiveness, change, resourcing and reputation — yet 80% had had no training in it. The tactics they met most often were networking, using key players to back initiatives, befriending power brokers, bending the rules and self-promotion. Rarer, but not unknown: misinformation, rumour-spreading and keeping "dirt files". Buchanan summarised the attitude he found as "you stab my back, I'll stab yours".

The lesson for a marketing leader: opting out of politics is not a neutral choice. It hands influence to others.

### Political skill with integrity: owl, fox, donkey, sheep

Simon Baddeley (University of Birmingham) and Kim James (Cranfield) describe political behaviour on two dimensions: **reading** (how aware you are of the informal workings of power) and **carrying** (whether you act with integrity or play psychological games for personal gain). The four combinations are memorable:

| | Acts with integrity | Plays games for self-interest |
|---|---|---|
| **Politically aware** | **Owl (wise):** reads the system, shares information, looks for outcomes that work for the organisation | **Fox (clever):** reads the system and manipulates it |
| **Politically unaware** | **Sheep (innocent):** believes the best idea wins on its merits; ignores informal influence; often sidelined | **Donkey (inept):** self-interested but clumsy |

Many marketing specialists start in the sheep position: they assume a good plan will win on its merits. The aim is the owl — fully aware of how decisions are really made, and using that awareness openly for the organisation's benefit. The model is a practitioner framework from UK management education, widely used and memorable rather than empirically validated.

### Sources of power

French and Raven (1959; US), with Raven's later addition, describe six bases: **legitimate** (formal role), **reward**, **coercive**, **expert**, **referent** (being liked and identified with) and **informational** (controlling or explaining information). Scale-up marketing leaders usually have little legitimate power over sales or product and rely on expert, informational and referent power. Customer insight and pipeline data are informational power — but only once translated into the founder's and the board's language.

### Which tactics work

Gary Yukl's research programme (US) identified eleven proactive influence tactics, including rational persuasion, inspirational appeals, consultation, collaboration, exchange, coalition, legitimating and pressure. Falbe and Yukl (1992) found inspirational appeals and consultation most effective at gaining commitment, and pressure, legitimating and coalition tactics least effective. Rational persuasion is especially important when influencing upwards. Kennedy, Fu and Yukl's (2003) study across twelve cultures found rational persuasion, consultation and collaboration rated effective in every country studied; Lee and colleagues' 2017 meta-analysis broadly confirms the pattern.

### Persuading across European cultures

Erin Meyer (INSEAD) places countries on a "persuading" scale. Italy, France and Spain sit at the **principles-first** end: people want to understand the reasoning, the method and the counter-arguments before accepting a conclusion. The US, Canada and Australia sit at the **applications-first** end: lead with the example and the practical outcome. The UK, Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden sit between. For a London marketing leader presenting a plan to a French co-founder or a German investor, the fix is often to add the "why" and the method before the recommendation — while for a US investor, lead with the result and the case study.

### Principles of persuasion and behavioural design

Robert Cialdini's principles (reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, and unity; US) describe how people respond to requests. Some underlying studies have faced replication questions; use them as heuristics. The UK Behavioural Insights Team's **EAST** framework — make it **Easy**, **Attractive**, **Social** and **Timely** — is a practical British tool for designing internal adoption (for example, getting sales to use a new lead-scoring process).

### Stakeholder mapping

Mendelow's power–interest grid sorts stakeholders into **manage closely** (high power, high interest), **keep satisfied** (high power, low interest), **keep informed** (low power, high interest) and **monitor**.

### The ethical — and legal — limits

Persuasion appeals to someone's reasoning and genuine interests; manipulation bypasses or exploits them. Three tests:

1. **Transparency:** would I be comfortable if they knew exactly what I was doing and why?
2. **Organisational benefit:** is this good for the company and its customers, not just for marketing or for me?
3. **Reversibility:** would I accept this tactic being used on me?

For marketing leaders in the UK, the line between persuasion and manipulation is also a legal line in customer-facing work. The Competition and Markets Authority's discussion paper on **online choice architecture** (2022) set out how design can harm consumers — false urgency, false scarcity, misleading defaults. Since April 2025, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 has allowed the Authority to enforce consumer law directly, with fines of up to 10% of global turnover, and it has named misleading urgency and scarcity claims and fake reviews among its priorities. A leader who would never use false scarcity on a customer should apply the same standard internally: inventing a competitor threat to win a budget argument fails all three tests.

## 2. Practical applications for scale-up marketing leaders

### Influencing the founder

Founders vary in how much control they want to keep. Noam Wasserman's research on 212 US start-ups (*Harvard Business Review*, 2008) describes a "rich versus king" trade-off: founders who prioritise control behave differently from those who prioritise the company's value. Diagnose which you are dealing with. With a control-oriented founder, influence through consultation and by framing proposals as extensions of their vision; with a value-oriented founder, rational persuasion with commercial evidence travels further. Either way, agree decision rights explicitly: "Which marketing decisions do you want to make, which do you want to be consulted on, and which are mine?"

### Budget cases with finance and the board

In a scale-up, the budget is tied to runway and the next funding milestone. Lead with rational persuasion in the board's language — pipeline, customer acquisition cost, payback period, contribution to the next round's targets — and consult the finance lead on how they want to see it.

Two UK sources help the argument:
- **Brand versus activation.** Les Binet and Peter Field's work for the LinkedIn B2B Institute (2019), based on Institute of Practitioners in Advertising effectiveness data, found business-to-business effectiveness was maximised at roughly 46% brand-building and 54% activation. The optimum varies by category and stage; treat it as the start of a conversation about the cost of cutting brand entirely, not as a rule.
- **Market context.** The Institute of Practitioners in Advertising's Bellwether Report for the third quarter of 2026 (published 15 October 2026) found UK companies revised marketing budgets upwards at the strongest rate in more than two years, with growth in all seven categories, while executives remained cautious about their financial prospects. Use current Bellwether data, not last year's, when arguing about what peers are doing.

### Alignment with sales on lead definitions and pipeline

Use collaboration and consultation, not legitimating ("the process says…"). Co-design the definition, agree joint metrics, review conversion by source monthly. In early-stage companies the sales team is often as new as marketing; build the definition together rather than inheriting one.

### Launch prioritisation with product

Combine inspirational appeals (the customer and market story) with rational persuasion (market size, competitor timing, pipeline at stake). Build a coalition only if consultation fails — coalition tactics are among the least effective and are easily read as fox behaviour in a small company.

### Agencies and freelancers

Use exchange and collaboration: scope, term, performance incentives. Avoid pressure; it produces compliance and weaker work. A small, volatile scale-up client has less leverage than it assumes; the best partners choose their clients too.

## 3. Diagnostic questions

1. Am I more owl, fox, donkey or sheep in this company? Where do others think I sit?
2. Which of my six power bases are strongest here? Which am I relying on too heavily?
3. Is my founder more "rich" or "king"? How does that change my approach?
4. Who are my "manage closely" stakeholders, and when did I last consult rather than inform them?
5. Which tactics do I default to with the founder, finance, sales and my team? Are they the effective ones?
6. For my key stakeholders, are they principles-first or applications-first?
7. Have I used a tactic in the last month that would fail the transparency test?

1. Place yourself and your three main stakeholders on the owl–fox–donkey–sheep grid; note one behaviour that would move you towards owl.
2. Map stakeholders for one live decision on the power–interest grid (20 minutes).
3. Replace one planned "inform" email with a "consult" conversation.

## 6. Evidence notes, scale-up and European variation

- **Strong:** influence-tactic findings (multiple studies, a meta-analysis and a twelve-culture study), though mostly survey-based rather than experimental. **UK survey evidence:** Buchanan (2008). **Practitioner model:** owl–fox–donkey–sheep. **Contested in parts:** some studies behind Cialdini's principles. **Investor-funded research:** Binet and Field's business-to-business work (LinkedIn) uses the Institute of Practitioners in Advertising's case database, which over-represents successful campaigns. **US:** Wasserman's founder research.
- **Scale-up:** power concentrates in the founder, the lead investor and whoever controls the runway forecast; influence is personal and fast; coalition-building is visible and risky in a small company. Experiments are persuasive: a two-week test often beats a slide.
- **Larger company (for contrast):** influence runs through committees, budget cycles and formal coalitions; stakeholder mapping is more complex; legitimate power counts for more.
- **European variation:** principles-first versus applications-first reasoning (Meyer); comfort with open disagreement; and, in more hierarchical cultures, the importance of securing the most senior person's agreement first.
- **Regulated sectors:** compliance and legal hold real veto power over campaigns. Consult them early; treat them as stakeholders to manage closely, not obstacles.
