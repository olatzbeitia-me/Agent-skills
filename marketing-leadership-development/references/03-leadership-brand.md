# Module 03: Crafting a leadership brand

## 1. Key concepts

### Leadership brand

Ulrich and Smallwood (2007; US) define a leadership brand as the identity a leader becomes known for: the link between the results they deliver and how they deliver them, anchored in what stakeholders value. Marketing leaders already know a brand is a promise kept consistently. A leadership brand applies the same test to yourself: it is proved by behaviour, not slogans.

### Strengths: two instruments worth knowing

- **Strengths Profile** (Cappfinity, founded by Alex Linley, formerly the Centre of Applied Positive Psychology, UK). Assesses 60 strengths against three criteria — performance, energy and use — and sorts them into four groups, each with its own advice:
  - **Realised strengths** — you perform well, they energise you, you use them often: *marshal* them.
  - **Unrealised strengths** — you perform well and they energise you, but you rarely use them: *maximise* them.
  - **Learned behaviours** — you perform well but they drain you: *moderate* them.
  - **Weaknesses** — *minimise* their impact rather than trying to fix them.

  The "learned behaviours" category is particularly useful in scale-ups, where generalist marketing leaders often become very good at things that exhaust them (reporting, operations, chasing approvals). A brand built on learned behaviours is a brand you will not sustain.
- **VIA Survey of Character Strengths** (VIA Institute on Character; Peterson and Seligman, 2004; US). Twenty-four character strengths under six virtues. Free to take; detailed reports are paid. Gallup's CliftonStrengths is a widely used proprietary alternative whose research is mainly published by Gallup.

All three are self-report; pair them with external evidence (below).

### What the evidence says about strengths use

- Rudolph and colleagues' 2025 meta-analysis found using character strengths at work is related to work performance and wellbeing.
- Vásquez-Pailaqueo and colleagues' 2025 meta-analysis of strengths-based *interventions* found moderate gains in work engagement (d = 0.37) and smaller gains in job performance (d = 0.27), from only eight controlled studies.

Conclusion: using strengths more is reliably associated with better outcomes; interventions help modestly; the intervention evidence is still small.

### Reflected Best Self

Roberts, Spreitzer, Dutton and colleagues (2005; US) suggest asking 10–20 people for stories of when you were at your best, looking for patterns, and writing a short self-portrait. It produces *external* evidence of strengths, which matters because internal and external self-awareness diverge (module 02).

### Linking strengths to style

Strengths shape your natural style. Curiosity tends towards coaching and involving leadership; perseverance and self-regulation towards goal-oriented or pacesetting; hope and creativity towards engaging or visionary leadership (see Dulewicz and Higgs in module 01). Overused strengths become weaknesses: curiosity becomes indecision; perseverance becomes stubbornness with agencies; a bias to action becomes chaos for the team. Lead your brand with strengths you can deploy deliberately.

### Leadership value proposition

Adapting Osterwalder and colleagues' *Value Proposition Design* (2014; Swiss authors): for each key audience, identify their "jobs" (what they are trying to achieve), "pains" and "gains", then show how your strengths relieve those pains and create those gains. For a scale-up founder, the job might be reaching the next funding milestone; the pain, a pipeline they cannot predict; the gain, a marketing leader who brings an honest forecast and a plan before being asked.

### Why it matters in scale-ups specifically

The ScaleUp Institute's 2021 survey found scale-up leaders strengthening their senior teams sought people who had **grown a business** (34%) much more than people from a **larger corporate** (10%). The same survey series consistently lists brand building, marketing and communications among the skills most needed in scale-up leadership teams. In a scale-up market, a leadership brand that says "I have helped a company grow through the next stage" is worth more than one that says "I have led large teams" — and leaders coming from corporates need to make that translation explicit, with evidence.

## 2. Practical applications for scale-up marketing leaders

- **Audiences are different from a large company.** In priority order, usually: the founder or chief executive; the board and investors; the sales and product leads; the team you are building; candidates you want to hire; the market (customers, partners, the trade press). In a scale-up, the marketing leader's external brand is also a recruiting asset — good marketers join leaders they have heard of.
- **Brand attributes that tend to matter in scale-ups:** commercial honesty ("tells us what isn't working before we ask"), speed with judgement, building a team from scratch, making a small budget go a long way, and connecting brand to pipeline.
- **With finance and the board:** no surprises; clear trade-offs; numbers that reconcile with finance's.
- **With sales:** "marketing that makes this quarter easier and next year's pipeline bigger".
- **With the team:** develops people quickly; fair between creative and analytical specialists; protects focus.
- **External channels:** LinkedIn, speaking at industry and investor-community events, podcasts, and contributions to professional bodies (the Chartered Institute of Marketing, the Institute of Practitioners in Advertising, the Marketing Society). In regulated sectors, check your company's social media and disclosure policy first; in scale-ups, also check what the founder is comfortable with you saying about growth figures.

## 3. Diagnostic questions

1. What are my realised strengths, and which of my most-used skills are actually learned behaviours that drain me?
2. Which strengths have I overused in the last year?
3. If I asked five colleagues to describe me in three words, what would they say? What do I want them to say?
4. What is the one thing the founder, the head of sales and my team would each say I am reliably good for?
5. If I am coming from a corporate, what evidence do I have that I can help a company grow through its next stage?
6. Which audience matters most to my next career step, and what is their biggest pain?
7. In my priority channel, what do people actually see of me?

## 4. Templates

### Template A: Leadership value proposition

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Realised strengths (3–5, with evidence) | e.g. Curiosity (two colleagues' Reflected Best Self stories cite my questioning of attribution assumptions) |
| Learned behaviours to moderate | e.g. Detailed reporting — delegate to the analyst |
| Core values (3) | |
| Priority audience | e.g. Founder and board |
| Their jobs | Reach next funding milestone; predictable pipeline |
| Their pains | Marketing spend they can't explain to investors; surprises |
| Their gains | A marketing leader who brings the forecast and trade-offs first |
| How my strengths relieve pains and create gains | Curiosity: I learn their model before arguing for mine. Honesty: I report under-performing channels first |
| Proof points | e.g. Cut cost per qualified opportunity by moving spend from two weak channels to one strong one, in one quarter |
| One-sentence value proposition | "I help [audience] achieve [job] by [distinctive, strength-based approach], so that [outcome]." |
| Brand boundaries (what I won't be) | e.g. "I won't defend spend I can't explain." |

*Artefact:* a one-sentence value proposition per priority audience, with proof points.

### Template B: Channel visibility audit (one priority channel)

| Indicator | Current (last 90 days) | Target (next 90 days) | Improvement action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reach** — relevant audience who saw my contributions (e.g. LinkedIn impressions from target sectors; attendance at my all-hands slot) | | | |
| **Relevance** — share of contributions linked to my value proposition | | | |
| **Engagement quality** — substantive comments, questions or messages from my target audience (not likes) | | | |
| **Consistency** — contributions versus plan | | | |
| **Conversion** — concrete outcomes (speaking invitations, inbound candidates, investor or founder requests for my view) | | | |
| **Alignment** — does what I say publicly match how I behave in meetings? (ask two colleagues) | | | |

Channel notes:
- **LinkedIn:** use the platform's analytics for impressions and audience profile, but treat vanity metrics with caution; one message from a target founder or investor outweighs hundreds of likes from peers.
- **Internal (all-hands, written updates, board sessions):** track follow-up requests and whether the founder or board repeat your framing.
- **Investor-community and industry events:** track repeat invitations and post-event conversations that continue.
- **Candidates:** track inbound applications that mention you.

*Artefact:* a completed audit with three improvement actions and a 90-day review date.

## 5. This week

1. Take the free Strengths Profile starter assessment or the VIA Survey (about 15–20 minutes), or send a short Reflected Best Self request to five colleagues.
2. List two learned behaviours you use daily, and one way to hand each over or reduce it.
3. Draft your one-sentence value proposition for your single most important audience.
4. Complete the "current" column of the visibility audit for one channel, and make one contribution there that reflects your value proposition.

## 6. Evidence notes, scale-up and European variation

- Research on strengths *use* is encouraging but mostly correlational; evidence for strengths *interventions* rests on a small number of controlled studies. All strengths instruments discussed are self-report; Strengths Profile and CliftonStrengths are proprietary, with much validation published by their owners.
- "Personal brand" advice in popular media is mostly opinion. The skill grounds it in Ulrich and Smallwood's results-plus-attributes logic, and in behaviour.
- **Scale-up:** the founder's brand often dominates; the marketing leader's brand may need to be internal first, then external once the founder is comfortable. External visibility doubles as a hiring asset.
- **Larger company (for contrast):** a leadership brand must travel across divisions; consistency and internal sponsorship matter more than external profile.
- **European variation:** self-promotion norms differ. British understatement can read as lack of conviction in some markets, while visible self-promotion can read as arrogance in the UK and Nordic countries. Adjust the register, not the substance.
- **Regulated sectors:** external visibility is constrained by financial promotion rules and company policy; internal channels and professional bodies matter more.
