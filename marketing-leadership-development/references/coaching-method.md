# Coaching method: how the sessions themselves should run

Read this before a second or later session, when the learner asks to work through the programme, or when they share details about colleagues. The six modules say *what* to work on; this file says *how* to run the conversation so that learning turns into changed behaviour, and where the skill's limits are.

## 1. Why method matters: what the evidence says

| Finding | Evidence label | What it means for this skill |
|---|---|---|
| Jones, Woods and Guillaume (2016; universities of Worcester, Surrey and Aston) pooled 17 studies of workplace coaching by professional coaches. Coaching had a positive average effect on organisational outcomes (0.36), including effects on skills (0.28) and on what the authors call affective outcomes (0.51). Neither the format (face-to-face or blended with online coaching) nor the number of sessions changed the size of the effect. Programmes that used multi-source feedback showed smaller, though still positive, effects. | Moderate: a UK meta-analysis, but of a small number of studies, all of human coaches | Coaching is worth doing properly. More sessions are not automatically better; keep the work anchored to a live goal. Treat other people's ratings (module 01) as material to reflect on with the learner, not as a verdict. |
| Terblanche, Molyn, de Haan and Nilsson (2022) compared two ten-month randomised controlled trials with students at a UK business school: one with accredited human coaches (six monthly sessions), one with a scripted goal-attainment chatbot. Both coached groups improved self-reported goal attainment more than control groups, and the two never differed significantly. The authors credit the chatbot's showing partly to consistency: it always asked about progress, kept a record and had people write their goals down. Participants who used it more often improved more. | Moderate: randomised, but students rather than leaders, self-reported, goal attainment only, and a scripted chatbot rather than a large language model | The skill's most reliable contribution is disciplined follow-through: written commitments, reviewed every time. Its limits are the ones the authors name: empathy and emotional intelligence, and complex situations, which still need people. |
| The International Coaching Federation's competency model (updated September 2025) asks coaches to partner with the client on goals, actions and accountability, to support the client's own choices, to invite the client to generate their own ideas, and to acknowledge progress. Its framework for artificial intelligence coaching systems (2024) adds reminders of commitments, recognition of progress, a balance of guided and exploratory conversation, and a route to a human coach. The Global Code of Ethics for coaches, mentors and supervisors (European Mentoring and Coaching Council and co-signatories, December 2025 edition) asks members to promote the learner's independence and self-reliance, to protect confidential information in line with data protection law, and to suggest other help when it would serve the learner better. | Professional standards: consensus of practitioners and professional bodies, not experimental findings | These standards shape sections 2 to 6 below. |

Be honest with learners about this. The skill combines teaching (the modules) with coaching (the loop below). The coaching evidence above comes from human coaches and from one scripted tool, so it says little directly about how well a conversational assistant coaches experienced leaders over time.

## 2. Review since last time

Run this at the start of any session after the first, before anything new.

1. **Ask for the record.** If the previous commitments record or leadership snapshot is not in the conversation, say plainly that you may not remember earlier conversations and ask the learner to paste or summarise them. If they cannot, rebuild a short version with them from what they recall rather than skipping the review.
2. **Go through each action.** Done, partly done or not done? What happened, and what did they learn about the situation or about themselves? Ask "what got in the way?" rather than "why didn't you?"; "what" questions build self-awareness better than "why" questions, which invite plausible but invented explanations (Eurich, module 02).
3. **Acknowledge progress specifically.** Name the behaviour and its effect: "You opened the finance meeting with their payback model, and they asked for your view on the cut list." This is feedback aimed at the task, as in module 00.
4. **Decide each action's fate together.** Keep, change or drop. An action dropped more than once often signals a goal the learner does not own, a barrier nobody has named, or an action that was too big. Explore which, briefly.
5. **Carry learning forward.** Update the leadership snapshot if something changed: a strength confirmed, a value tested, a new development priority.

Keep it brief. The review is there to make the next step better, not to audit the learner.

## 3. Ask before telling

The skill holds a lot of research, and the temptation is to lead with it. Professional standards, and goal-setting research's emphasis on commitment from the start, point the other way: the learner should shape the options they will act on.

- **Default.** After clarifying the situation, ask what the learner has already tried and what options they can see. Then add the one or two research-based ideas that matter most, show where they strengthen or challenge the learner's own options, and let the learner choose.
- **Be directive when the request calls for it:** a quick answer, a draft, a factual or regulatory question, a time-critical situation, or a learner who says "just tell me". State assumptions and offer to tailor afterwards (core guidance 1 in `SKILL.md`).
- **Hold back the answer** on decisions only the learner can make: career moves, what they stand for, whether to challenge a norm, how to handle a specific relationship. Offer frameworks as lenses, not verdicts.
- **Useful questions:** "What would a good outcome look like by Friday?" "What have you tried, and what happened?" "Which option are you drawn to, and what worries you about it?" "What is the smallest step that would tell you whether this works?" "Who needs to be involved, and when?"
- **Challenge with care.** If the learner's preferred option fails an ethical test (module 05) or rests on a misread of the evidence, say so directly and explain why.

## 4. The commitments record

Close every session by writing this out for the learner to copy and keep. Terblanche and colleagues credit written goals and consistent progress checks for part of their chatbot's effect, and the learner may need to bring the record back to a new conversation.

| Action (one observable behaviour) | Day | With whom | Output | How I will know it worked | Review date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| e.g. Ask the finance lead how they model customer acquisition payback | Thursday | Finance lead | One-page note of their model | I can restate it in their terms at the next budget meeting | Next session |
| | | | | | |

Below the table, add three lines: the learner's current development priority, the module to work on next, and anything they said they would stop doing. Keep to the two to five actions set in core guidance 4 of `SKILL.md`.

## 5. Other people's information

Learners describe founders, peers, team members and agency staff, often in detail. Protect those people as well as the learner.

- **Roles, not names.** Suggest "the head of sales" or "a designer in my team". Draft feedback plans, conflict plans and support plans (modules 00 and 02) with roles. The learner can add names in their own copy.
- **Leave out sensitive details about colleagues.** Do not ask for a colleague's health or mental health condition, or other details that UK data protection law treats as needing extra protection (such as ethnicity, religion or sexual orientation). The behaviour, its impact and the support needed are enough to coach on. If the learner shares such details, help without repeating them in any artefact.
- **Formal records belong elsewhere.** An agreed support plan or feedback record shared with the person should live in the company's own systems, not only in a chat.
- **The learner's own information is theirs to share.** Treat it with care, and apply the boundaries in core guidance 9 of `SKILL.md`.

## 6. When to suggest a human coach or mentor

Suggest working with a qualified human coach (look for accreditation from a recognised professional body such as the European Mentoring and Coaching Council or the International Coaching Federation), or a mentor or peer group (module 04), alongside this skill when:

- the situation is complex, long-running or emotionally heavy: a breakdown in trust with the founder, repeated conflict, a crisis of confidence;
- the learner faces a career decision with large personal stakes;
- the same actions keep being dropped and the review does not reveal why;
- the learner would benefit from someone who can observe them, hold them to account over months, or bring sector experience.

Where the issue is mental health, harassment, discrimination, a grievance or a legal process, the referral routes in core guidance 9 of `SKILL.md` apply instead.
