# Module 04: Networking and relationship building

**What this module covers:** the core elements and value of networks for individuals and organisations; analysing the structure and diversity of a network; practical network-building techniques; and designing a network strategy aligned with long-term career and organisational goals.

## 1. Key concepts

### Three kinds of network

Herminia Ibarra and Mark Hunter (2007; INSEAD) distinguish:

- **Operational** networks — for getting today's work done.
- **Personal** networks — for development and support, often outside the organisation.
- **Strategic** networks — for future direction and for support from people who shape it.

Their finding: managers moving into leadership over-invest in operational networks and neglect strategic ones. In a scale-up the strategic network is mostly *outside* the company — investors, non-executive directors, peer leaders, future hires.

### How many relationships you can actually keep

Robin Dunbar (University of Oxford) describes social networks as layers of roughly 5, 15, 50 and 150 people, with emotional closeness and contact time concentrated in the inner layers. Relationships in the outer layers fade without contact. The practical point for a busy leader: you cannot maintain hundreds of active relationships, so a network strategy is about choosing which 50 to invest in, and keeping a light touch with the rest.

### Weak ties and bridges

Granovetter (1973; US) argued acquaintances bring new information because they move in different circles. The strongest test is Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson and Aral's 2022 *Science* paper: randomised experiments on LinkedIn's "People You May Know" algorithm across more than 20 million people over five years. They found:

- **An inverted U.** Weaker ties help job mobility, but only up to a point; moderately weak ties (measured by mutual connections) helped most.
- **Industry matters.** Weak ties helped more in more digital industries; strong ties helped more in less digital ones.

Zachary Neal's response in *Science* argued the results show the value of *bridging* ties rather than weak ties as such. The practical message survives either way: your most valuable connections are often acquaintances one step removed, who connect you to a world you do not already know.

### Brokerage

Ronald Burt (US) shows that people who bridge otherwise disconnected groups get early access to diverse information and more credit for good ideas. In a study of 673 managers in a large US electronics company's supply chain, those whose networks spanned "structural holes" were more likely to have ideas judged valuable (Burt, 2004). A scale-up marketing leader sits naturally between product, sales, customers, the founder and outside partners — a brokerage position, if used.

### Diversity, sponsorship and fairness

Uzzi and Dunlap (2005; US) recommend building networks through high-stakes shared activities — cross-functional projects, industry working groups, boards of charities or professional bodies — rather than events where you meet people like yourself.

UK research adds a fairness dimension. Friedman and Laurison's *The Class Ceiling* (2019) found informal sponsorship helped explain why people from privileged backgrounds progressed faster in television and other elite occupations; the Bridge Group's financial services research called for transparent sponsorship and project allocation. Networks are not neutral. A leader who builds their own network should also open it to people in their team who lack one.

### Energy, overload and generosity

Rob Cross and colleagues (US) found that people who leave others feeling energised are more central and higher-performing in organisational networks, and that collaborative demands concentrate on a few people (Cross, Rebele and Grant, 2016). Adam Grant's *Give and Take* (2013; US) suggests generous people who protect their time do well over the long run; treat it as a well-argued synthesis rather than a single robust finding.

### Networking discomfort

Casciaro, Gino and Kouchaki (2014) reported that instrumental professional networking made people feel "dirty", reducing how often they networked. A pre-registered replication of the original first study (Ziani, 2022 preprint) found no effect, so treat the finding as contested. The authors' practical advice still helps people who dislike networking: focus on learning, look for shared interests, and think about what you can give.

### What UK scale-up leaders value

The ScaleUp Institute's annual surveys consistently find peer networks, mentors and non-executive directors among the most valued forms of support for scale-up leaders. In the 2022 review, half of scale-up leaders had a mentor and 92% of those found the guidance valuable. Leaders also asked for peer networks to extend beyond chief executives to the wider senior team — which includes marketing leaders.

Index Ventures notes the growth of **fractional executives** — experienced operators working part-time across several start-ups before a full-time leader is hired. For marketing leaders, fractional chief marketing officers are both a network to learn from and, at certain stages, a career option.

## 2. Practical applications for scale-up marketing leaders

- **Your strategic network is external.** Map investors (and their portfolio platform teams), non-executive directors, peer marketing leaders at companies one or two stages ahead, fractional executives, agency founders, and early customers who will speak for you.
- **Use investor networks.** Many venture firms run portfolio communities for functional leaders; ask the founder for an introduction. These give you peers who face the same stage problems.
- **Peer groups one stage ahead.** The most useful peer is a marketing leader at a company 12–24 months further along. They can tell you what breaks next.
- **Internal brokerage.** Run a short monthly "customer signal" session that brings product, sales and customer success together around what customers are saying. It makes you the broker.
- **Agencies and freelancers.** Know senior people at two or three agencies beyond your current roster; it improves your negotiating position and access to talent in a market where you may need to scale quickly.
- **Your team's networks.** Introduce your analyst to the finance lead and your designer to customers. Building their networks builds your brokerage and helps close the sponsorship gap.
- **New markets.** Expanding into Germany, France or the Netherlands? Build a small network there before launch — a local marketer, a partner, a customer — and expect relationship-building norms to differ (Meyer's *Culture Map* describes task-based versus relationship-based trust).

## 3. Diagnostic questions

1. What share of my last month's work conversations were operational, personal and strategic?
2. Of my 15 most important contacts, how many know each other? (High overlap means low diversity.)
3. Who do I know at a company one or two stages ahead of mine?
4. Do I have a mentor, a peer group, or a relationship with a non-executive director? If not, why not?
5. Who in my network bridges to a world I don't understand — investors, finance, technology, another country?
6. When did I last help someone with no immediate return? When did I last open my network to someone in my team?
7. Who would I call if my role were eliminated after the next funding round?

## 4. Templates

### Template A: Network mapping exercise

Step 1. List 20–25 people you have relied on, or who have influenced your work, in the past 12 months.

Step 2. For each, record:

| Name | Type (operational / personal / strategic) | Function, sector or investor | Inside or outside the company | Tie strength (1 weak – 5 strong) | Overlap with my other contacts (high / medium / low) | Energy (+ / 0 / −) | What I give | What I get |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Step 3. Analyse:
- **Balance:** what share is strategic? Leaders moving up typically need at least a quarter.
- **Stage coverage:** do you know anyone one or two stages ahead? Anyone who has been through a down round, a layoff or an acquisition?
- **Diversity:** functions, sectors, countries, levels, genders, ages, backgrounds. Anyone from finance, investors, customers?
- **Brokerage:** where are the gaps between clusters that only you bridge? Where are you missing bridges?
- **Strength profile:** enough moderately weak ties (people you know through one or two mutual connections)?
- **Reciprocity:** relationships where you only take?

*Artefact:* a network map (table or simple cluster drawing) and a one-paragraph diagnosis.

### Template B: Twelve-month network strategy

| Element | Content |
|---|---|
| Career goal (2–3 years) | e.g. Chief marketing officer of a business-to-business software scale-up at 200–500 people |
| Company goal I serve | e.g. Make marketing the most predictable source of pipeline before the next round |
| Network gaps the goals reveal | e.g. No peers one stage ahead; weak with investors; nobody in the German market |
| Target relationships (5–8 names or roles) | |
| Shared-activity route for each | e.g. Investor portfolio community; professional body working group; co-host a customer roundtable |
| What I can give each | Insight, introductions, customer data, speaking slots, mentoring |
| People in my team whose network I will open up | |
| Relationships to reduce or hand over | |
| Monthly rhythm | e.g. Two new strategic conversations, one reconnection, one give-first introduction |
| Review indicators | New strategic contacts; introductions received; opportunities surfaced |

## 5. This week

1. Complete Steps 1 and 2 of the network map (45 minutes).
2. Ask the founder or an investor for one introduction to a marketing leader at a company a stage ahead.
3. Make one give-first introduction between two people who would benefit from knowing each other.
4. Reconnect with one dormant, moderately weak tie in a sector, function or country you want to understand.

## 6. Evidence notes, scale-up and European variation

- **Strong:** the LinkedIn weak-ties experiment (large-scale and randomised, but one platform and focused on job mobility). **Strong but mainly correlational:** Burt's brokerage findings. **Well established, general:** Dunbar's layers. **Contested:** the "networking feels dirty" effect. **Synthesis:** Grant's giver findings. **Survey data:** ScaleUp Institute findings on mentors and peer networks describe what leaders value, not what works best.
- **Scale-up:** the strategic network is external and investor-shaped; peers one stage ahead are the highest-value contacts; job security is lower, so a network is also a safety net.
- **Larger company (for contrast):** the strategic network is mostly internal — across divisions, regions and the executive committee.
- **European variation:** trust-building norms differ; in more relationship-based cultures, time spent outside the meeting agenda is part of the work, not a distraction from it.
- **Regulated sectors:** take care over gifts and hospitality (for example under the UK Bribery Act 2010) and over conflicts of interest with agencies and suppliers.
