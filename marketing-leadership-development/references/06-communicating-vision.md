# Module 06: Communicating vision

## 1. Key concepts

### Answer first

Barbara Minto's *Pyramid Principle* (developed while she was at McKinsey in London) says to start with the answer or recommendation, then the supporting arguments, then the evidence. Her situation–complication–question structure frames the opening. Senior readers decide in the first few lines whether to keep reading.

### Plain English, the British way

- **The GOV.UK style guide** (Government Digital Service) recommends short sentences, common words, active voice, and writing for the task the reader needs to complete. It has been refined through user research on how people actually read government content.
- **The Plain English Campaign** offers similar guidance and a long record of before-and-after examples.
- **George Orwell's six rules** ("Politics and the English Language", 1946) remain the shortest summary: avoid stale metaphors; prefer short words; cut words you can cut; prefer the active voice; avoid jargon when an everyday word will do; break any rule sooner than write something barbarous.

Clarity is a form of respect for the reader, and also a test of whether you have decided what you think.

### Authenticity and identity during growth

Vision lands when it matches the leader's behaviour and admits uncertainty honestly — the authenticity point of the trust triangle in module 00. Over-polished, slogan-heavy or AI-smoothed messages damage trust.

Scale-ups add a specific challenge. Peter Kalum Schou's 24-month study of a science-based venture (*Journal of Management Studies*, 2023) found that as the company adopted a commercial logic to satisfy investors and customers, its organisational identity fragmented — people lost a shared sense of who they were. Vision communication in a scale-up is therefore not a one-off launch; it is a continual, open reworking of "who we are and why" as the company commercialises and its headcount doubles.

### Story structures for strategy

- **Situation–complication–resolution** (Minto): suited to board papers and investor updates.
- **"What is" versus "what could be"** (Nancy Duarte, *Resonate*, 2010; US): move between the current reality and the better future, ending with a concrete picture of success.
- **Strategic narrative** (Andy Raskin; US practitioner): name a big shift in the world, show that there will be winners and losers, describe the "promised land", introduce the capabilities that get you there, present evidence. Widely used for investor and sales narratives in technology scale-ups.

### What makes spoken messages land: British research

Max Atkinson's studies of British political speeches (*Our Masters' Voices*, 1984; *Lend Me Your Ears*, 2004) found that audiences respond most reliably to a few rhetorical devices — notably **contrasts** ("not this, but that") and **lists of three** — which signal a point is complete and worth reacting to. Used sparingly, they make a key line memorable; overused, they sound like a politician.

Two further ideas help with memorability:
- **Sticky ideas** (Chip and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick*, 2007; US): simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, told as stories.
- **Peaks and endings.** Research by Daniel Kahneman and colleagues on the "peak–end rule" found people's memories of experiences are shaped disproportionately by the most intense moment and the ending. Applying it to presentations is a reasonable extension rather than a tested finding, but a deliberate high point and a strong close cost little.

### Presentation craft

- **Signposts:** tell the audience where you are ("first… second… finally…").
- **Interaction:** questions, a quick show of hands, a two-minute pair discussion. They keep attention and turn listeners into participants.
- **Endings:** finish with the decision or action you need, not "any questions?". Take questions before the final summary.

### Evaluating AI in professional communication

The evidence is promising but uneven:

- **Experimental evidence (US).** Noy and Zhang (2023, *Science*) gave 453 professionals access to ChatGPT for writing tasks: time fell by 40% and quality rose by 18%, with the biggest gains for weaker writers. Dell'Acqua and colleagues (Harvard Business School and Boston Consulting Group, 2023) found consultants using GPT-4 did much better on tasks inside the AI's capabilities, but were 19 percentage points less likely to reach a correct answer on a task outside them — and the AI's output was persuasive even when wrong. Doshi and Hauser (2024, *Science Advances*) found AI help made individual stories more creative but all the stories more alike — a direct risk for brand distinctiveness.
- **UK government evidence.** The Government Digital Service's cross-government trial gave Microsoft 365 Copilot to 20,000 civil servants from 30 September to 31 December 2024. Participants reported saving an average of 26 minutes a day, estimated from self-reported time bands. A separate HM Revenue and Customs evaluation, which allocated 3,000 licences randomly, found participants self-reported saving 2–3% of their working week (around an hour a week). Both are self-reported; the gap between them shows how much results depend on the task and the people.

**Practical lesson:** use AI for drafting, summarising, restructuring and variations; keep humans responsible for judgement, facts, claims, voice and anything regulated.

### The rules: UK and EU

- **EU Artificial Intelligence Act.** If a UK scale-up sells into or operates in the EU, two obligations matter to marketing leaders now. An **AI literacy** duty (Article 4) has applied since 2 February 2025: organisations using AI systems must ensure staff have sufficient AI literacy. **Transparency obligations** (Article 50) have applied since 2 August 2026: people must be told when they are interacting with an AI system such as a chatbot; providers of generative AI tools must mark outputs in a machine-readable way; organisations using AI must disclose deepfakes; and AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be disclosed unless it has been through human review and someone holds editorial responsibility for it. The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force from 27 July 2026) delayed the high-risk obligations but did not delay Article 50; generative systems already on the market before 2 August 2026 have until 2 December 2026 to add machine-readable marking. Check with legal advisers how this applies to your company.
- **UK.** The Information Commissioner's Office publishes guidance on AI and data protection. The Advertising Standards Authority's codes apply to advertising however it was produced; AI-generated claims must still be truthful and substantiated.

### The professional introduction

A concise introduction (30–60 seconds) states who you are, the value you bring (your leadership value proposition from module 03), one piece of evidence, and where you are heading — adapted to the audience.

## 2. Practical applications for scale-up marketing leaders

- **Board papers and investor updates.** Answer first; the commercial impact in the first paragraph; marketing metrics in an appendix. One recommendation, two options, the trade-off, the risk.
- **All-hands.** As headcount doubles, a third of the room may be new. Repeat the core story every time, explicitly. Use one customer example and one number.
- **Written updates.** Scale-ups increasingly run on written communication across time zones. Write so that someone who joins next month understands the strategy without you in the room.
- **Reworking identity.** When the company's story changes — a pivot, a new market, a shift from product-led to sales-led — say so openly, explain what is staying the same, and give people a chance to ask. Schou's research suggests silence here lets identity fragment.
- **Agency and freelancer briefs.** Put "what we need to achieve" in one sentence at the top.
- **AI in the marketing team.** Agree a short team policy covering what data may be entered into which tools, who checks facts and claims, how brand voice is protected, and when AI use must be disclosed — including the EU rules if you operate there. This also meets the spirit, and possibly the letter, of the AI literacy duty.
- **Creative and analytical audiences.** Creatives respond to the story and the customer; analysts to logic and evidence. Good vision communication offers both: a story with a spine of evidence.
- **Multi-country audiences.** For principles-first audiences (France, Spain, Italy), explain the reasoning before the conclusion; for applications-first audiences (US), lead with the result (Meyer; see module 05).

## 3. Diagnostic questions

1. Could a busy founder or investor get my main point from the first two lines of my last update?
2. Can I state the marketing vision in one sentence, without jargon?
3. What is the "what is versus what could be" contrast at the heart of our strategy?
4. Has our company's identity changed in the last year? Have I said so out loud?
5. What did my last presentation's audience remember? How do I know?
6. Where am I using AI in a way that would fail an accuracy check or make us sound like everyone else? Do we operate in the EU, and does our team know the rules?
7. Does my introduction say what I do, or what I make possible?

*Example (adapt; figures illustrative):* "I'm Sam, head of marketing at a 120-person business-to-business software company in London. Most companies at our stage struggle to show investors what marketing contributes to pipeline. My approach is to start with finance's model, not marketing's, and to bring the trade-offs before anyone asks. Last year that let us move a fifth of our paid budget into the channels that measurably grew qualified pipeline. I'm now building a team that the business sees as its most predictable source of growth — I'd be interested in how you're approaching that here."

## 5. This week

1. Rewrite the opening of your next important email, update or paper so the answer is in the first two lines.
2. Draft your vision story (Template A) for one audience, including what stays the same.
3. Run the AI check on one document you drafted with AI help; if you operate in the EU, confirm your team knows the Article 50 basics.
4. Write and say out loud your 60-second introduction. Time it and cut it to 60 seconds.

## 6. Evidence notes, scale-up and European variation

- **Practitioner methods with strong face validity:** answer-first, GOV.UK style and plain English. They have not been tested experimentally as packages; the GOV.UK guide is informed by extensive user research.
- **Observational research:** Atkinson's work on speeches analyses real audience responses rather than controlled experiments. **Extension:** applying the peak–end rule to presentations.
- **AI evidence:** the experiments are rigorous but short-term, task-specific and based on early models. The UK government trials are large but self-reported. Re-evaluate as tools change.
- **Regulation:** the EU dates above reflect the position as of October 2026; check for changes before relying on them.
- **Scale-up:** vision is often carried by the founder; the marketing leader translates and repeats it. Identity is fragile during growth; communicate changes to "who we are" openly.
- **Larger company (for contrast):** vision cascades through layers, so simplicity and repeatability matter even more, but identity is more stable.
- **European variation:** principles-first versus applications-first audiences; differing comfort with bold claims and self-promotion; translation and local examples matter more than polish.
- **Regulated sectors:** external communication needs compliance review whether AI-assisted or not.
