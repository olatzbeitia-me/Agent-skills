# Demand proxies: share of search, in-market buyers and buying situations

## 1. Share of search

**What it is.** A brand's share of the organic Google searches made for all the brands in its competitor set: searches for the brand divided by searches for every brand in the set. Les Binet presented it at the Institute of Practitioners in Advertising's EffWorks conference in 2020 as a fast, free measure built on Google Trends data.

**Why it earns a place.** Most public signals show activity. Share of search is one of the few with published evidence linking it to commercial outcomes:
- Binet tested it in 3 categories (cars, household energy and mobile handsets) and found it tracked market share in each. In cars it gave warning of market share changes up to a year ahead, and he described the gap between share of search and market share as an especially solid indicator of where market share is heading.
- The Institute's share of search think tank (2021) pooled 30 case studies from 12 categories and 7 countries. On average share of search came out at about 83% of a brand's market share, with the ratio varying by category and country. Brands whose share of advertising spend exceeded their market share tended to see share of search rise.
- Both are clear that these are correlations, not proof of cause. Binet adds that turning searches into sales depends on other factors, price above all, and that not every search is a good sign: complaints and crises generate searches too.

**Method (Google Trends, trends.google.com).**
1. Fix the competitor set first: the brands a buyer in the given industry would actually compare, drawn from the competitors found in your research. The measure is only as meaningful as the set, so state it.
2. Put every brand in a single comparison so that all values share one scale. Google's help says the current Explore view takes up to 8 groups of terms with up to 50 terms in each; the classic view takes 5 groups of up to 25.
3. Add each brand's common variants to its group (alternative spellings, abbreviations, the name with and without its main product). Trends does not merge misspellings, spelling variants, synonyms or singular and plural forms. Use quotation marks for an exact multi-word name.
4. Where Trends offers a topic for the company, it groups searches for that concept across languages. For a brand name that is also an everyday word, use the topic or pair the name with a category word, and say which you chose.
5. Set the region to the markets checked, select web search, and choose a period long enough to smooth. The think tank recommends a rolling average over 6 to 12 months, so 2 years or more of data is a sensible minimum.
6. Download the data, work out each brand's share in every period (its value divided by the sum of all the brands' values) and apply the rolling average.
7. Read the direction and the standing: is the company's share rising, flat or falling, and where does it sit against the set? If a credible source publishes the company's market share, compare the two: that gap is the signal Binet found most telling (share of search above market share points to growth, below it to decline). Label the reading as inferred.

**Limits and traps.**
- Trends reports relative values on a 0 to 100 scale, calculated from a sample of searches. They are not search counts. Never present them as search volumes.
- Low-volume terms show as 0, and Google adds a little random noise for privacy that is most visible on low-interest terms. Many business-to-business brands and niche categories fall below the line. Record that as "not measurable: search volume too low", never as zero demand.
- One-off spikes from news, a crisis or a launch distort short periods, and Google warns against reading a spike as popularity. Smooth the series and name any spike you see.
- Do not convert share of search into a market share estimate. The 83% figure is an average across very different categories and countries.
- Google describes Trends as not a scientific poll and as one data point among others. Treat share of search as one proxy alongside the rest of the audit, not a verdict on its own.

**How to report it.** Observed: the values, with the terms or topics, region, period and date retrieved, plus the comparison link so the reader can rerun it. Inferred: what the trend suggests about brand demand, with the confidence level and the reason.

## 2. In-market and out-of-market buyers

**The idea.** Professor John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute set out the "95-5 rule" for business-to-business categories in 2021. If companies replace a provider about once every 5 years, around 20% of them are in the market over a year and roughly 5% in any quarter, so about 95% are not buying right now. With a 2-year purchase cycle the figures become about 50% a year and around 13% a quarter. Dawes calls the 95% a heuristic rather than a precise rule: the real share depends on how often the category is bought.

**What follows.** Advertising mostly reaches people who will not buy soon. Dawes argues that it works mainly by building and refreshing memories that connect the brand to the category, so the brand comes to mind when a buyer later enters the market. Marketing aimed only at buyers ready to purchase now competes for a small group and does little for the next one, and the research he cites finds that less familiar brands win less consideration and fewer clicks.

**Using it in the audit.**
1. Estimate the category's purchase cycle from what is visible: contract lengths on pricing pages, renewal terms, or norms stated by a credible industry source. Present it as an assumption, and work out the rough in-market share with Dawes's arithmetic (1 divided by the years between purchases gives the yearly share; about a quarter of that gives the quarterly share).
2. Sort the company's visible activity into two groups. Capture: search ads, retargeting, demo-request and trial offers, review-site and comparison listings. Creation: brand advertising, video, category education, thought leadership, events and sponsorships, and leaders' social presence.
3. Judge the balance against the purchase cycle. In a long-cycle category, a programme made almost entirely of capture is a finding: the company reaches the small in-market group and is largely invisible to everyone else. Label the judgement as inferred, because public data shows activity, not how the budget is split.
4. Do not invent a "right" ratio for the company. None of the sources here sets one.

## 3. Buying situations (category entry points)

**The idea.** Professor Jenni Romaniuk of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, in a 2022 paper published by LinkedIn's B2B Institute, describes category entry points as the cues buyers use to retrieve brands from memory when a buying situation arises: a need, a trigger, a moment or a context. She reports that brands linked to a wider and fresher set of these situations achieve higher acquisition and retention.

**Mapping them from public evidence.** Measuring which brands buyers actually remember needs a survey of category buyers, which a public-evidence audit cannot run. What the audit can map is which buying situations the company's messaging is trying to claim:
1. List the situations named or implied in the ads, homepage, landing pages and leaders' posts.
2. Look for gaps using Romaniuk's prompts, from the point of view of both the business and the person buying: when, where, while doing what, alongside which other products, why, how the buyer feels, and with or for whom. Fictional examples: "when the finance team needs to close the month faster"; "while replacing an ageing system"; "something the board will trust".
3. Compare with competitors' messaging: which situations several brands already claim (crowded), which nobody claims (open), and which this company covers alone.
4. Weigh each gap with Romaniuk's 3 tests: credibility (would buyers believe it, and does the product deliver it), competitiveness (how many brands already hold it) and commonality (how often the situation arises, or how much it is worth when it does).

**Limits.** Messaging coverage is not memory. Say the company "targets" or "talks about" a situation, never that it "owns" one, unless a published survey of buyers shows it. Mark any prioritisation of gaps as inferred.
