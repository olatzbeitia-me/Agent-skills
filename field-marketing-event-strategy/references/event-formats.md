## 1. Quick comparison

| Format | Primary job | Cost per head | Best when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive dinner | Open senior doors, accelerate open deals | Very high | Deal sizes justify it and sales has named accounts to invite |
| Roundtable or breakfast briefing | Peer discussion with senior buyers | High | Senior audience short on time; strong topic; good in the UK and Northern Europe |
| Field seminar or roadshow | Create pipeline in mid-market accounts at scale | Medium | Target accounts cluster in a few cities |
| Workshop or hands-on lab | Move technical evaluators forward | Medium | Product needs to be seen working; evaluation stage is the bottleneck |
| Customer advisory board or user group | Expansion, retention, references | Medium to high | Installed base large enough to matter |
| Side event around a major show | Harvest the show's audience without stand costs | Medium | Buyers travel to the show; company is a challenger or new entrant |
| Flagship user conference | Category leadership, expansion, partner ecosystem | Very high | Large installed base; later-stage companies |
| Trade show stand | Volume, visibility, new accounts | High, with hidden costs | Target-account density is high and buyers come to shop |
| Conference sponsorship or speaking | Credibility and conversations | Variable | Speaking slot available, or a package that creates real conversations |
| Webinar | Coverage, education, nurture, pre- and post-event support | Low | Regions or segments where in-person spend is not justified |
| Partner-led event | Borrowed audience, shared cost | Low to medium | Strong partner with overlapping customers |

## 2. Hosted events

You control the guest list, which is why hosted events usually produce better pipeline per pound than external ones.

**Executive dinner (12 to 20 guests).** Good for senior buyers and deals in flight. What good looks like: a topic rather than a pitch, a respected facilitator or customer speaker, one company host per table, seating planned so each prospect sits near someone useful (a customer advocate or the right specialist), and a defined next step for every guest. Pitfalls: no-shows are common for free invitation-only dinners, so invite more people than seats, confirm 48 hours before, and keep a waitlist. Pitching from the front ruins it.

**Roundtable or breakfast briefing (10 to 25).** Peer discussion on a problem the buyer has, often under the Chatham House Rule (what is said can be used, but not attributed). Works well with senior UK and Northern European audiences who will give up an early slot but not an evening. Needs a sharp question, not a product theme.

**Field seminar or roadshow (40 to 150).** A half-day of content in a city where target accounts cluster. Useful for mid-market pipeline at scale. Needs a credible external speaker or customer to draw attendance; vendor-only agendas struggle.

**Workshop or hands-on lab.** For technical evaluators. Shortens evaluations because people use the product rather than hear about it. Keep groups small enough for real help.

**Customer advisory board or user group.** Primarily for expansion and retention, but customers who attend become references, and inviting a few late-stage prospects to a user group can be powerful.

**Side event around a major show.** A reception, breakfast or private dinner near the venue, timed outside the main programme. Captures the show's audience without the stand cost. Check the show's rules: some organisers prohibit competing events during official hours or restrict them to sponsors.

**Flagship user conference.** Only when the installed base is large enough to fill it with customers; otherwise it becomes an expensive vendor showcase. Usually a later-stage company's play.

## 3. Trade shows (exhibiting)

Earns its place when target-account density is high and buyers attend to compare suppliers. Gives volume and visibility, and in some sectors absence is noticed.

What good looks like:
- A modest stand built around conversations, with a meeting space, rather than a large stand built around a brand statement.
- Most of the value comes from meetings booked before the show; the stand is the meeting point.
- Badge scans are not leads. Qualify on the spot (see `sales-plays.md`) and route only real conversations to sales.

Pitfalls: the package price is a fraction of the true cost. Add stand build, furniture, power, internet, rigging, cleaning, material handling, lead capture licences, travel, accommodation and staff time. A rough planning rule many teams use is that all-in cost lands at two to three times the space price, but verify against the exhibitor services manual for the specific venue.

## 4. Conferences (sponsoring and speaking)

**Speaking slots** are usually the best value at a conference: credibility with the room and a reason to follow up. A customer co-presenting is far stronger than a vendor alone. Submissions often close many months ahead, so check calls for papers early.

**Sponsorship:** pay for things that create conversations (hosted lunch tables, a meeting room, a facilitated session, a breakfast briefing in the programme) rather than logo placement. Ask exactly what attendee data you receive and on what lawful basis.

## 5. Webinars and virtual events

Their job in an event strategy:
- **Coverage:** regions and segments where in-person spend is not justified, including local-language sessions.
- **Pre-event:** warm up the target account list and give sales a reason to get in touch.
- **Post-event:** turn the event's content into nurture for people who did not attend or did not engage.
- **Deep dives** for evaluators already in the pipeline.

What good looks like: a panel with a customer or analyst rather than a product walkthrough, a time slot that suits the region, and follow-up that covers both live attendees and those who watch on demand, which is often the larger group.

Pitfall: on their own, webinars rarely source enterprise pipeline. Treat them as nurture and acceleration unless the company's own data says otherwise.

## 6. Partner-led events

Co-hosted with technology partners, resellers or consultancies. Shared cost and a borrowed audience, which suits new entrants and regions where the company has no direct sales team. Agree in writing before the event: who owns which leads, how leads are shared, who follows up and by when, and how the shared costs split. Unclear lead ownership is the most common reason partner events produce nothing.
