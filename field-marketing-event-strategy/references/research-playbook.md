## 1. Company research

| Source | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Company website: product and solutions pages | What it sells, and which sectors it prioritises (solution pages by industry are a strong signal of focus) |
| Customer stories and logo walls | Ideal customer profile by size, sector and region; which personas are quoted (those are the buyers and champions) |
| Pricing page | Price point and packaging, and therefore rough deal size and whether the motion is self-serve or sales-led |
| Events and webinars pages | The current programme: what it hosts, what it attends, how often, in which languages |
| Careers page and job adverts | Where it hires account executives and sales development representatives is where it intends to sell. Field marketing job adverts often name target events and regions. Adverts quoting quota or "six-figure deals" hint at deal size |
| Press releases and news | Funding, acquisitions, new regional offices, partnerships, launches; anything that changes where it needs pipeline next year |
| Annual reports and investor materials (public companies) | Revenue by region and segment, stated growth priorities |
| Review sites (G2, Gartner Peer Insights, TrustRadius) | Competitors in its category, reviewer job titles (personas), reviewer company sizes |
| LinkedIn company page | Headcount by region, events it promotes, executives who speak |
| Competitors' events pages and sponsor lists | Which events the category treats as essential, and where an absence would be noticed |
| Partner pages and marketplaces | Technology alliances, cloud marketplace listings, systems integrators; partner events are often the cheapest route to a qualified audience |
| Speaker appearances | Where its executives already speak; existing relationships with organisers |

Searches to adapt (replace the brackets):

- `[company] customer story` / `[company] case study [sector]`
- `[company] pricing`
- `[company] webinar` / `[company] events 2027`
- `[company] sponsor` / `[company] exhibitor` / `[company] booth`
- `[company] competitors` / `[company] alternative`
- `[company] new office [region]` / `[company] expands [country]`
- `[company] hiring account executive [city]`
- `[company] partner [technology or consultancy]`
- `[competitor] sponsor [event]`

## 2. Inferring what is not published

Label all of these **Inferred** and show the reasoning.

- **Deal size:** pricing page tiers multiplied by realistic seat or usage counts for the typical customer; review-site comments about cost; job adverts quoting deal sizes; for public companies, revenue divided by customer count.
- **Sales cycle:** enterprise sales-led motions with procurement and security review typically run several months to a year; self-serve or low price point products run in days or weeks. Job adverts sometimes state cycle length. Say which category the company falls into and why.
- **Purchase interval:** how often a typical customer buys, or reviews its supplier for, this kind of product. Look for the usual contract term (pricing page, order form, terms of service, public filings), review-site comments about switching, and renewal language in customer stories. Feeds the in-market check in `measurement.md`.
- **Buyers:** personas quoted in customer stories, reviewer job titles, the titles the company targets in its own webinars, and the job titles in its adverts for sales roles ("selling to chief information security officers").
- **Regional priority:** where sales roles are being hired now matters more than where the website says it has offices. A region with no sales hires and no partners cannot follow up an event.

## 3. Event landscape research

Work region by region.

1. **Start from the buyer.** Ask what the persona attends: their professional body, sector association, regulator's conference, analyst events, community meetups. Category vendor shows are a secondary list.
2. **Search broadly, then narrow:**
   - `[sector] conference [country] 2027`
   - `[persona job title] summit [city]`
   - `[category] expo [country]`
   - `[sector] association annual conference`
   - `[analyst firm] summit [region]`
3. **Use event directories as a cross-check, not a source of truth:** general directories such as 10times, and national trade fair calendars (for German trade fairs, the AUMA database). Directory dates are often out of date; confirm on the organiser's site.
4. **For each candidate, open the organiser's site and collect:** dates and venue, the sponsorship prospectus (packages, prices, deadlines), last year's exhibitor and sponsor list, agenda and speaker list (speaker employers and titles show audience seniority), audience breakdown by job title, sector and company size.
5. **Competitor check:** search competitor names against exhibitor and sponsor lists.
6. **Target-account density test:** if the user supplies a named account list, cross-reference it against previous speakers, sponsors and any published attendee companies. If not, use speaker and sponsor companies plus the organiser's audience breakdown as a proxy, and say it is a proxy.
7. **Record what you could not find.** "No exhibitor list published" is useful information.

## 4. Red flags

- No published agenda or speakers within a few months of the event.
- No previous exhibitor or sponsor list, and an audience described only as a headline total.
- Speaking slots sold as part of a package with no audience data. Paid speaking is not always bad, but it needs evidence of who is in the room.
- Event series that run the same format in many cities where the audience is mostly other vendors. Check the speaker list for buyers.
- Dates or venues that keep changing, or an organiser with no track record.
- A prospectus that promises "leads" or "attendee lists". Under UK and EU data protection law, attendee contact details can only be passed on with a lawful basis, usually consent, so check what is actually delivered.
