## 1. Why lead counts mislead

- **Purchases are decided by groups.** Forrester's 2026 survey of nearly 18,000 business buyers found that, on average, 13 people inside the buying organisation and 9 outside it influence a purchase, with larger groups for expensive or complex purchases. Procurement acts as a decision-maker in 53% of buying cycles. An event that reaches one person per account has reached a small part of the decision.
- **Opportunities take longer than a financial year.** Forrester's event research warns against treating one attendee as the single source of an opportunity, and notes that enterprise opportunities can take years to become revenue, so an in-year return on investment calculation will usually understate what an event did.
- **Leadership is less convinced than event teams.** In Forrester's 2026 survey of business events, about half of event leaders were satisfied with how their events performed, against about a third of chief marketing officers. The programmes that close that gap report contribution to revenue, customer growth and buying-group engagement, not activity such as badge scans.

What this means for the plan: the unit of planning and reporting is the target account and its buying group, and the reporting window has to match how long buying takes.

## 2. The in-market check

**Purpose.** Step 2 works out how many qualified opportunities an event must create to pay its way. This check asks whether that many new accounts can be buying at all during the reporting window.

**The evidence.** John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute shows that most business buyers are out of the market at any one time. Where companies replace a supplier about once every 5 years, only about 20% are in the market over a year and about 5% in a quarter. He presents the "95% not in market" figure as a heuristic rather than a precise rule, and gives a simple method: divide the period by the average time between purchases (a 2-year interval gives 50% over a year and about 13% in a quarter).

**Steps.**

1. **Estimate the purchase interval:** how often a typical customer buys, or reviews its supplier for, this kind of product. Evidence: the usual contract term (pricing page, order form, public filings), review-site comments about switching, the user's own renewal data, or a short customer survey asking how often they buy. Label it Confirmed, Inferred or Assumed.
2. **Take out accounts that are already in the market or follow their own calendar.** Accounts with an open opportunity are counted under acceleration; existing customers follow their renewal and expansion dates.
3. **Work out the in-market share for the window:** months in the window divided by months in the purchase interval, capped at 100%.
4. **Multiply by the new target accounts the team will realistically meet:** booked meetings and hosted-event guests, not the organiser's headline attendance.
5. **Compare the result with the qualified opportunities the economics require.**

**Worked illustration** (fictional figures; replace them with the company's own). The £60,000 trade show in step 2 needs 6 to 10 qualified opportunities. The team expects to meet 40 new target accounts, and customers in this category typically review their supplier every 4 years.

- Next 3 months: 40 × 3 ÷ 48 = about 2.5 accounts in the market.
- Next 12 months: 40 × 12 ÷ 48 = 10 accounts in the market.

The show only pays its way if almost every account that comes into the market over the next year becomes a qualified opportunity for this company. That is not a credible 90-day target. The options are to lower the tier, to raise the number of new target accounts met for the money (hosted side events, more pre-booked meetings), or to keep the event for a stated longer-term job and judge it on that job.

**Reading the result.**

- Required opportunities comfortably below the 12-month figure: the near-term pipeline target is credible.
- Close to it: a stretch. Say so in the plan.
- Above it: the event cannot pay its way on new-account pipeline in that window. Downgrade it or reframe its job.

**Cautions.**

- People who travel to a buyer-focused show, or accept a meeting, are probably more likely than average to be in the market, so the base rate may understate the share among the accounts met. Treat the result as a conservative sense check, not a forecast.
- The arithmetic treats purchases as spread evenly through the year. Contract end dates and budget cycles bunch them; where renewal dates are known, use them instead.

## 3. Buying-group coverage

**Definitions.** Forrester separates a buyer's persona (their job and title) from their buying role (the part they play in this decision). It names 5 common roles: champion, influencer, decision-maker, user and ratifier (a professional purchaser or negotiator, often procurement). Before outreach starts, agree with sales a buying-group profile for the product: the expected size of the group, the roles, and the job titles that usually fill them.

**Planning.**

- **Invite by role, not only by seniority.** Formats reach different roles: dinners and roundtables suit decision-makers and champions, workshops and labs suit users and technical evaluators, and partner and analyst events reach the advisers outside the account who influence the decision.
- **Give buyers outside voices to check claims against.** Forrester reports that buyers check what artificial intelligence tools tell them against peers, product experts and analysts, and are more likely to approach a provider because of what an industry expert said than because of what an artificial intelligence tool said. That supports the existing preference in `event-formats.md` for customer speakers and peer-led formats over vendor presentations.
- **Plan for procurement.** With procurement deciding in more than half of buying cycles, follow-up should give the champion material they can take to procurement: the business case, references, and answers to the usual commercial and security questions.

**Measuring.**

- **Buying-group coverage** for each target account: how many of the roles in the profile the team met, hosted or spoke with, out of the roles expected. Report how many target accounts reached 1 role, 2 roles, and 3 or more, rather than a total lead count.
- **Deduplicate people, not accounts.** Forrester warns that later contacts from an account are often discarded as duplicate leads although they are members of the buying group, and that several engaged people from one account signal buying activity far better than one person's behaviour. Attach each person to the account and to any open opportunity.
- **Record the buying role** on the contact and, where there is an opportunity, on the contact's role in that opportunity.

## 4. Reporting windows by the event's job

Set the window when the event's job is set (step 2), and write it into the agreement with sales.

| The event's main job | Window for the pipeline verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Accelerate open deals | Within a quarter of the event | Forrester (SiriusDecisions) guidance: acceleration of late-stage opportunities can be measured within a quarter |
| Create pipeline in new accounts | 6 to 12 months, longer for enterprise sales cycles | The same guidance gives 6 to 12 months for early-stage demand creation; Forrester notes enterprise opportunities can take years; the in-market check shows how few new accounts can buy in any one quarter |
| Expand existing customers | Against each customer's renewal and expansion dates | Expansion follows contract dates rather than the event date |
| Build credibility in a category, or enter a new market | Leading indicators first (target accounts reached, buying-group coverage, speaking slots, partner commitments), with a 12-month pipeline review | Most accounts reached are not yet in the market, so pipeline arrives well after the event |

Keep the 30-, 90- and 180-day reviews in `sales-plays.md` for every event, but do not let a 90-day view be the verdict on an event whose job is new accounts.

**A fairer comparison.** Over the same window, compare target accounts the team met with comparable target accounts it did not meet. Accounts that accept meetings differ from those that do not, so the comparison shows association, not proof of cause; say so when reporting it.

## 5. Making the customer relationship management system see the effect

Attribution settings decide what gets counted. Check them before the event, not when the report looks thin.

**Salesforce** (Customizable Campaign Influence, the version Salesforce recommends):

- The influence models look for campaign members who also hold a **contact role on an open opportunity** and create influence records from that link. Records can also be added by hand or through code, but the automatic route depends on contact roles, so event contacts at accounts with open or new opportunities need adding to the opportunity as contact roles, ideally with their buying role. No new influence records are created once an opportunity is closed.
- **Every campaign member counts, whatever their member status,** unless the auto-association rules filter by status. Salesforce's own trade show example filters on a responded status. Without such a rule, people who were only invited are credited alongside those who attended.
- The **auto-association time frame** is the maximum number of days between the date a contact was added to the campaign and the date the opportunity was created. Contacts are often added at invitation, weeks before the event, so the clock starts early. Set the time frame to cover the pre-event period plus the reporting window for the event's job; a short time frame silently drops the new-account opportunities that arrive later.

**Other systems,** including HubSpot, have their own attribution models and windows. Check them in the same way and write down the settings next to the results, so nobody compares numbers produced under different rules.

## 6. Limits of the evidence

- Forrester's survey figures are averages across countries, sectors and purchase types. The full reports are for clients; the public summaries give headline figures only.
- The 95-5 figure is a heuristic, and purchase intervals vary widely by category. The method matters more than the number.
- The SiriusDecisions reporting windows date from 2017 and are practitioner guidance, not measured benchmarks.
- Salesforce behaviour depends on edition and on how the company has configured it. Confirm the company's actual settings with its marketing operations team.
