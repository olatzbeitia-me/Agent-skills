# Market research and idea development

## Part 1: Researching the market

Work through these in order. Each section ends in one or two *insights*: a finding plus why it matters for advertising. A statistic on its own is not an insight.

### 1. The business
- Website: proposition, products and tiers, pricing (and any free plan), proof points, calls to action (book a demo, start free, buy now), customer logos and case studies.
- Recent moves: funding, leadership changes, launches, partnerships, research it has published, events it attends. These often signal where the company is heading and what it wants to be known for.
- Filings and press: size, ownership, growth.
- How it sells: self-serve, demo-led, field sales, partners. This decides which channels and conversion points make sense.

### 2. The category and its direction
- Size and structure from credible sources: official statistics, regulators, industry bodies, listed competitors' reports. Note the date of every figure.
- Forces changing the market now: regulation, funding, technology adoption, cost pressures, consolidation. Ask which of these change *what buyers need* or *how urgently*.
- Where the category is on its adoption curve. A market where most buyers have never bought the product needs education; a market where most already have one is about switching, and the advertising should differ accordingly.

### 3. Customers: how they buy
- **Who decides and who influences.** In business-to-business, name the roles (owner, operations lead, finance, frontline users) and what each cares about.
- **Triggers.** What makes someone start looking: a deadline, an inspection, growth, a failure, a new hire, a price rise, a life event.
- **How they search and compare.** Search terms (Google's own tools if available; Google Trends for relative interest, seasonality and regional differences, labelled Observed); review sites; directories; communities and forums; trade shows; peers.
- **Their language and objections.** Review sites (Trustpilot, Google reviews, G2, Capterra, app stores), forum threads and community groups show how buyers describe the problem and why they hesitate. Paraphrase; don't copy.
- **Where they spend attention.** Which platforms, publications, events and communities. Use Ofcom for broad UK media habits; sector bodies and trade media for professional audiences.

### 4. Moments and seasonality
Recurring moments that change demand: budget cycles, financial year-end (April in much UK public funding), regulatory deadlines, inspection cycles, trade show calendars, school terms, retail peaks. Ideas timed to these usually outperform evergreen ones.

### 5. Regulation and constraints
What advertising in this category may and may not say or target: ASA and CAP Code, sector regulators, platform policies (financial services, health, special ad categories). Note anything that rules out a channel or shapes the message.

## Part 2: Developing the ideas

### Types of idea
Spread ideas across types so the set isn't just "use channel X":
- **Channel**: a channel or placement that suits these buyers for a reason found in the research
- **Audience**: a segment that is underserved, newly emerging or poorly targeted by competitors
- **Message or angle**: a truth about the buyer or market that competitors aren't using
- **Format or creative**: a format that suits how these buyers consume or decide
- **Offer**: what the ad invites people to do (a tool, a research report, a workshop, a free tier, a migration guarantee)
- **Timing**: built around a moment found in step 4
- **Learning**: a small test that would answer an important open question

### What each idea needs
1. **The idea** in one or two plain sentences.
2. **The evidence** it rests on, linked: the specific ads (with their library links, shown as screenshots in the competitor section) and the source pages.
3. **Channel and format, and why**: argued from how the channel works (its targeting, auction, formats, measurement), using the channel primer.
4. **What it would take**: creative, data, people or budget scale, in a line.
5. **How you'd know it was working**: the signal to watch, in a line.
6. **Caveats**: availability in the market, policy limits, assumptions it depends on.

### Fit checks before an idea goes in
- **Audience presence**: are these buyers actually on this channel in this market?
- **Economics**: does the business's price, margin or deal size support the channel's typical costs?
- **Sales motion**: does the conversion point match how the business sells?
- **Creative capability**: can the business realistically produce what the channel needs?
- **Measurement**: could anyone tell whether it worked?
- **Availability**: is the feature or channel live in this market today, confirmed at source?
- **Differentiation**: is it something competitors already do the same way? If so, what makes this version better?

An idea that fails audience presence or availability goes to "considered and dropped" with the reason.

### Ordering
Put the strongest idea first and say in a sentence why it leads (usually the best combination of evidence, fit and difference from competitors). This is a judgement about the ideas, not a schedule; don't add timelines, owners or effort scores.
